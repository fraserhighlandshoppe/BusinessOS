YOUR SYSTEMS: Session 4
Seeing Your Business As An Ecosystem
In this session, you'll learn how to keep your team motivated when progress is meaningful but "slow,"
how to identify the vital signs of your business (and report them eﬀectively), and how to balance the
ongoing war between "quantity" and "quality" in your business.

• Think of your business as an ecosystem. Just like most member of an ecosystem in nature can't
perceive the ecosystem as a whole, so too most people in the ecosystem of a business can't
perceive changes that are going on. And most of them fear change. So a question I ask myself is
"How can I show the change that is happening to my company, to the people who work inside of
it, in a meaningful way to them, and show them the ways it's positive?" For example, if your
business is growing 20% a year (that's faster than most businesses), that means it's growing less
than 2% a month, and on a daily basis the growth is so small you can't even see it. So to
communicate this growth in a meaningful way to your employees, you may need to really
magnify the chart so your team can see what's really going on.

• Here's another example:
Let's say you're focused on increasing your visitor to subscriber rate by 10%, and your subscriber
to customer rate by 10%. If you do both these things, it doesn't grow your business by 10%, it
grows it by much more because the eﬀects are compounded. Now, to the person who is just
working on the visitor to subscriber side to increase it by 10% over the course of a year, the small
improvements needed to get 10% are almost imperceptible. But if you show them the big
pictures, and how what they're doing rolls up into the bigger impact on the business, it makes a
big diﬀerence in their perceptions and motivation to "stay the course."

• An interesting phenomenon to look at in business is Parkinson's Law. It essential states that work
expands to fill time and to use up available resources. So if a team gets a bunch of resources, they
will use the resources up to get whatever result they were expected to get. Most of the time, they
won't "save" any of the resources. If you don't show how what they use up connects to the bigger
ecology - the larger business - they'll tend to continue to eat up resources. This is just part of
human nature - we get in our comfort zone and we don't think of the larger context unless
someone reminds us and wake us up.

• One of the things that's critical to do when you start a business is to shift your perspective so you
see your business as its own system and you standing outside of it. So you see your business as a
little ecosystem running on its own. One way to see it as an ecosystem is to use the analogy of the
human body. If you're not feeling well and you go to the doctor, the first thing they'll do is check
your vital signs. Why? Because they want to see how well your body "system" is working. What if
you had a set of metrics that were the "vital signs" for the ecology of your business? Think about it
for a moment. What does your business need to survive - what are it's "vital signs"? It could be
band deposits, new customers, etc.

• Many business don't focus on their "negative vitals." For example, it's easy to get sucked into
focusing on increasing your gross sales... but what about decreasing expenses? Often you have to
bring in 3-10x more in the door as what goes out. Bringing in more is often 1/10th as valuable as

• There's always a battle between quality and quantity. It's a battle we constantly fight in business.
How to manage the balance between quality and quantity, that's a big part of what business is
about. For example, you may want your customer service person to respond to customers in a
way that gets a high percentage of wow responses. But if she spends the whole day writing just
one awesome response, sure she may get a 100% wow score, but maybe there are 47 tickets she
never got to. The percentage of wows is a qualitative metric. But we also need the quantity to
balance it out - we need to know how many tickets she's responding to.

• Most of my friends who are very successful say don't take the money from investors. What you
have to pay down the road isn't worth it. If you talk to the Google's of the world, sure, they'll say it
was great. But the feedback I get from the people I know is that you have to be very careful taking
on investors. If you have vendors and you get into trouble with cash flow, you can call them up,
explain what's going on, and ask to be 30 or 60 days late... and it's usually no problem. But if you
call up the bank and tell them your loan payment is going to be late, they're not going to respond
in the same way. You can get in dangerous territory very quickly.
