YOUR SYSTEMS: Session 1
Introduction To Systems Thinking
In this session, you'll learn what Systems Thinking is, why it's important, and how to use it in your
business to get a 3-D view of the steps you must take to go to the next level of success.

• We’ll define a system as a group of parts and how they interact together. If we don’t understand
the system, then we can’t think at a level that could aﬀect the system, and instead we chase our
own tail, and wonder why every time we bite it… why someone else is biting us. If you don’t
understand a system, you can’t get there from here.

• As you hear me say a lot – my research shows me that most businesses, business mergers, etc., are
“failures.” They don’t go the way they were planned. 96% of businesses fail in the first 10 years.
Learning about systems and systems theories can help with these things because it allows us to
accomplish something that has only been a recent development in humans – that’s the ability to
zoom up a level and to think on the level of the whole system. Until recently, our thinking was
stuck in simple cause and eﬀect models. You push this rock, and it rolls down the hill. We couldn’t
see all the dynamics of systems.

• We’re going to talk about a type of system in business that’s becoming more popular, and that’s
the culture of a business. Systems thinking is very external, like looking at an ant farm or terrarium
from the outside. But a business culture is looking at a system from the inside, from within the
humans in the system. If you look at both levels – external and internal – you can get a lot of
valuable insights.

• If I’m trying to accomplish something, my perspective is that solving the problem is all about
figuring out how to think about it… how to “see” it. A lot of people try to figure something out
inductively – put together the pieces and figure out how it all fits together. But if you start with a
theoretical view of it from the beginning, and you develop the ability to create instances of that
theory, or overlay that theory on experiences you have in life, you get a 3-D eﬀect happen where
you see things diﬀerently than you saw them before. Now you understand how the thing works.

• This is how you become what I think of as a magician in the world – you make one small change,
and the whole thing shifts and everything starts working. Let's say you're a BMW trained
mechanic, working on high end cars for 20 years. When a car comes in, you use the computer,
make the fix, etc. But there’s something about an old farmer who, when he hears a funny noise in
the engine, he puts a broomstick on the motor, puts it up to his ear, all of a sudden he whack it
and it starts working. There’s a lot of power in having an intuitive feel for the system. A lot of
people go the route of becoming the 20 year certified BMW mechanic, learning how to use all
these external diagnostic tools, etc., instead of learning just a few theories that when you overlay
them over life – or in this case, you business – allows you to make a few small changes that have
huge leverage and result in big shifts with very little eﬀort.

• The way I see it, we humans are not wired or programmed for success in the modern business
environment, especially as business owners. My evidence for this is the simple statistics. If we
were wired to succeed, it makes sense that more than 50% of us would have successful

• Part of it is that large corporations are just better at hording all the value than individual humans
are. But it just seems we’re not wired or programmed to succeed. Humans tend to resist the things

themselves with certain people, they seem to be able to consistently get the outcomes they want.
This is called long term success. At the highest level, this is really systems at work. This is systems
thinking.

• Deming systemized his system thinking into several principles. One of the ideas he talked about a
lot is that the system is responsible for 94% of the results. Any of the parts is only 6 percent. So if
you have a team and things get screwed up, it’s the system's responsibility – meaning it’s YOUR
responsibility - because you hired the people, set expectations, etc. When something goes down,
ask how the system was the 94% of what happened, and how the person was only 6% of it.

• One interesting way to look at business systems is through the idea of the displacement of anger.
Imagine in a baboon troop, a dominant baboon and a lower ranking baboon walk by. The
dominant baboon makes a threatening display. What will the lower ranking one do? He may run
oﬀ, go straight to the oﬀspring of the dominant baboon, and make a threatening display. It seems
that we primates do that – we displace our anger and negative emotions down the hierarchy. In
our business, it’s relatively unnatural to say “How did I create the circumstances within which this
happened?” Maybe we created the culture. Ask yourself, “How would I need to make the system
look so that outcomes like this don’t happen?” You'll get diﬀerent answer than if you ask , “How do
I stop that person from screwing up?”

• Think of the system as the context, the ecosystem, the environment in which you’re operating.
