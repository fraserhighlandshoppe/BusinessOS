YOUR PEOPLE: Session 8
Incentives & Compensation
In this session, you’ll learn what psychological tests say about the negative eﬀects of incentivizing
behavior, why some people in your company will hate incentives and, whether you should have an
“open book” policy when it comes to compensation in your company.

• In psychology, they’ve done a lot of work in the area of incentives and come up with interesting
results. You can incentivize behavior, but when you do, you change the organism.

• In one psychological experiment, they took 2 groups of kids. They put one group in a room with
crayons. They told the kids if you color you get a gold star. The kids colored. With the second
group, they put the kids in a similar room with crayons, but didn’t mention to them any reward for
coloring. The kids start coloring anyways. Then they took both groups of kids and put them in
another similar room full of crayons and didn’t say anything to them. You know what happened.
The kids who weren’t incentivized before to color walked right in and started coloring. The kids
who were incentivized before didn’t color, most likely because they had been “trained” to expect
the incentive and now “needed” it to be motivated to color.

• Be careful with incentives. Almost everyone I know who has used incentives have horror stories to
tell. People who are in positions that traditionally have incentives are smart - they know how to
game the system in some way. The lower performers often think the incentive system is unfair
because the higher performers are getting paid more. You can manage this – just realize it will
take managing. Importantly, watch out that you’re disincentivizing without realizing it.

• The way to overcome all this stuﬀ is called “aligning incentives.” When you’re setting everything
up, make sure that the person going through the process has all the natural incentives in the
process aligned. For any approach you choose, there’s a tradeoﬀ.

• Another questions is the personal compensation for the business owner. Should you have an
“open book” about it? And how much “wealth” to show is too much? I think you can have a nice
house, nice car, and nice stuﬀ, as long as it doesn’t seem that you’re wildly throwing money away
(buying exotic sports cars, etc.)

• Ralph’s take on this topic:
You can’t measure before only with things like annual 360 assessments. There’s too much of a gap
between when the behavior happened and when the impact on the business occurred. At Coke,
one performance metric is cases per man hour. At Knott’s Berry Farm one metric is revenue per
guest, for example.

• When you think of metrics, think of something that everyone can understand, and it doesn’t
necessarily have to go to profit. If you do choose to share profit, you’re going to have to open the
balance sheets as well. You can’t go half way. You can’t jump the chasm in 2 steps. If you open the
books and they see you made $1 million profit, your employees may freak out and think if you’re
making all that money then why aren’t they getting paid more? But you then have to go to the
balance sheet side of things, and explain to them you have $50 million invested in the company,
and that they’ll actually make a better ROI in their 401k then you will in this business.
