Negotiating A Great Deal
When I teach people how to purchase properties I
teach them how to evaluate the property to insure
they don’t pay too much as well as to how to negotiate a really good deal. One of my students told
me how frustrated she was trying to find a good
cash flowing property based upon my teaching
and she actually began to wonder if I was realistic
with my expectations and then one day it happened. After running the numbers through the analyzer the results suggested this was a good deal.
She couldn’t believe it yet the numbers told the
truth and she ended up with two very good cash
flowing properties.
It is not uncommon for educated investors to
struggle to locate great cash flowing properties
because there are too many people willing to pay
more for a property than what it is worth based
upon the cash flow it generates. When you do find
a good cash flowing property you need to act
quickly in order to get it under contract before another investor makes an offer.
I was working recently with another student who
found a property over one year ago and after closer evaluation he determined that it was overpriced
so he decided to play the “wait and see” game.
This was a wise decision because over the course
of the past year the Seller has had several offers,
all of which failed simply because the property
was overvalued based upon the overall condition
and cash flow.
Eventually the Seller either becomes aware that
the price needs to be adjusted or they become
frustrated and perhaps even desperate to sell. After a price adjustment my student decided the
time was right to make an offer so he made an
offer for almost 10% below the current asking
price. My student didn’t have high hopes that his
offer would be accepted so when he received the
word that his offer was accepted he was very surprised and happy. The next step was to view the
property and actually inspect the property to see
what the real condition was. He quickly discovered
that the reason it hadn’t sold wasn’t just because
the property was overpriced but it was also in
great need of significant repairs and good management. And this was why the past deals failed.
The next step was to step back and look at all the
work required from a very practical perspective. In
a case like this when it is discovered that there is

significant work to be done
in order to bring the property up to market value he
needed to renegotiate the
purchase price. In sales
and negotiations there is a
saying that “the first person to speak loses” and
this is exactly what we did during the renegotiations. What my student did was complete a detailed list of all the major work required to be
done with an estimated cost. In this case the list
totaled just over $200,000 and when we sent
the letter to the Seller we simply asked what the
Seller was prepared to do. He would have been
very happy with a $100,000 price adjustment so
when we received word that the Seller would
reduce the price by $150,000 he was shocked!
To say he is happy would be an understatement. He now has the purchase price for 15%
below the asking price and the opportunity to
create over $300,000 in equity when he finishes
the rehab. If he had simply asked for what he
wanted ($100,000 price reduction) then he
would have likely only ended up with a $50,000
adjustment.
Knowing how to evaluate a property, negotiate a
fair deal and then renegotiate a great deal is a
very satisfying and exciting process which takes
time and patience. This is a big part of why I
love teaching how to buy real estate the right
way, helping my students find great deals and
save thousands and in this case hundreds of
thousands of dollars.

Know Your Limits, Seek Expertise
It was very interesting watching the reaction of my
fitness trainer when I mentioned real estate investing:
his voice inflection, his physical response to the
words “real estate investing”, his complete certainty
that this was a risky endeavour and never to be entertained as a viable option for him. He was wholly
vested in his belief that real estate is evil.
It was and is a purely emotional response to three
simple words: real, estate, investing. But why was so
much emotion involved
in the response? Why
was he trying to convince himself that real
estate is dangerous?
Where did this primal
reaction come from?
Why was he so desperately trying to convince
me that he was right?
After all, he’s trying to
convince the wrong person with this selfdelusion.
And I’m not about to try
and talk him out of this
limiting belief. He has
built a fortress of belief
around the topic of real
estate investing. Most
likely, this has come
from previous failed attempts or horror stories from
close family and friends. So my response was to ask
him if I could be a trainer like him. His response was
that it would take a minimum of thirty years including
training in medicine, kinesiology and nutrition. My
response to his explanation: this makes you an expert in your field. Beyond a doubt, following his training regimen produces results.
I then asked him if he had ever studied and practiced
real estate investing? Oh, yes, he went to seminars
facilitated by many self-proclaimed real estate gurus
but these programs are designed not to teach, but to
make profit for the organization. Then he did not truly
study real estate investing. Going to a seminar is not
learning about real estate. Going to a seminar is listening to a person lecture about real estate. How
would I fare if all I did was go to seminars to learn
about medicine but not ever practice it? Could anyone become an expert doctor just by going to seminars? I think he got it.
The reason why I sought him out over all other train-

ers is because of his results. I know that I am not
an expert in fitness. Otherwise, I would not be over
thirty pounds my ideal
weight when I hired him.
But this trainer’s regimen is tried and true. I saw my
colleagues become fit and healthy in six weeks. I see
my own body shed over twenty pounds in less than
four weeks, easily and effortlessly. This is his expertise. Why would he consider himself an expert
in real estate investing
if this is not his area of
interest or passion?
This is where he needs
to reflect on his strong
emotional need to avoid
real estate investing, at
all costs. I asked him to
reflect on why that is.
Because real estate is
the path to wealth creation and wealth preservation. This he
acknowledged. It is a
source of security and
stability for today and
for tomorrow. Again, he
agreed. And if he is so
afraid of real estate investing, why does he not seek
out an expert to invest for him? Real estate investing
may not be in his comfort zone but he does realize
that without it, his prospects for creating wealth have
remained limited. I invited him to stop fretting about
real estate investing on his own if it’s not his thing.
Know your limits and seek out expertise.

Decision #1 – Stress test all low
down/high ratio mortgages in
Canada.
Recall this is a purchase involving

speaking; renters will
never look after the home with the same level of
care that you would. That said, your renovations
should be tailored with the mindset that many
parts of the home will need to be:
a) Readdressed as renters move in & out
b) Primarily based on the length of time you plan
to keep the property.
It’s a delicate balance to do this properly. On one
hand, you want to attract the best possible
renters and be able to demand the highest rents
for the area to drive cash flow. On the other
hand, it makes little sense to spend on items that
will inevitably just need to be replaced due to
constant wear & tear from tenants.
The rule of thumb that I try to deploy is to buy the
top end products/materials for anything that I
know is going to be used more simply because
it’s a dual dwelling home. For instance, whenever I’m replacing hot water tanks I always tend to
go for the largest capacity tank I can get my
hands on with the best warranty & reputation for
quality. Reason being is on any given day there
could be up to 6-8 showers, 2-5 loads of laundry,
the dishwashers will run 2-4 times the list goes
on... That hot water tank is going to get a
workout! This is just one example of many where
you may want to consider spending more up-front
to minimize back-end costs and also provide your
tenants with the proper product for the type of
home their living in.
An example of an area where you’ll want to reduce costs is painting of interior walls. In my experience, even the absolute best tenants will
scuff up the walls enough that you’ll be forced to
repaint before the next tenant moves in. The
cost of paint can be anywhere from $20 /gal all
the way up to designer paint in the $80 /gal
range. Furthermore if you call a painter to do the
job their going to charge somewhere around
$2.75 /sq ft. You’ll soon find yourself spending

Page 9

thousands of dollars repainting each floor of
your rental property! A tip that I’ve used with
good success over the years is to never pay
more than $30 /gal for a decent quality paint
(any big box home improvement store should
have this) and then (if you don’t want to paint
it yourself) place an ad on your local free
classified website (Kijiji, Craigslist etc.) detailing the scope of work, a few pictures and the
amount your willing to pay. You’ll find there
are a lot of weekend warrior painters out
there more than willing to make a few extra
bucks. The level of quality may be lower than
your professional painters, but keep in mind;
you’ll be repainting those walls again when
the tenants eventually move on.
The lesson here is to be mindful of renovation
costs and always ask yourself when purchasing something for your rental,
Is this something I can see myself replacing
every year or will this last for the long haul?
Once you’ve determined that, you should
have a good idea of what type of budget you
should allow yourself on any given item.
Happy shopping!
