Why Motivation Matters
As the Real Estate investing expert your money partners will be looking to you for leadership, guidance and
motivation during both the good times and the bad
times.
This month’s article touches on Why Motivation Matters and comes to us from Richard Dolan, President of REIN and the resident expert on Performance
for real estate investors and professionals. You can
find Richard at www.reincanada.com.
As a professional real estate investor, I often find myself distracted, disengaged and disconnected from my
goals, aims and intentions to invest in real estate.
Somehow my ‘get up and go’ has ‘gotten up and gone’.
It begs the question: does motivation play a role in the
matter of realizing one’s real estate investment
goals and intentions?
To answer this question, consider how motivation is
defined as “the reason or reasons one has for acting or
behaving in a particular way; the general desire or willingness of someone to do something.”
The question for real estate investors is: what is
the reason or that something? To answer this question,
let’s first take a look at what exactly should motivate
you, according to Abraham Maslow’s hierarchy of
needs in his paper “A Theory of Human Motivation”. This hierarchy begins with the most basic motivation and moves up to the highest motivation, the motivation to self-actualize.
The Maslow Hierarchy of Needs:
 Physiological needs are considered the
basics and include air, food, water, sex, sleep, homeostasis and excretion.

When it comes to real estate investing, I personally
realized I invested, at first,
for the money. Then it became about the freedom.
Now I continue to push myself and my limits (both mentally and financially) for
what it means to me personally: the challenge, the
growth and the chance to say ‘I did it ‘cause I said I
would.’ But you aren’t me, obsessed with zerotolerance for playing small. So the question becomes: how do you motivate yourself in real estate
investing? Whether you’ve just gotten started, are
stuck or are feasting on the yield of average.
I would assert that the older we get, the less motivated we are because fewer factors motivate us.
Think about it. Growing up our parents held us up
from falling down. Teachers encouraged us along.
We played organized sports and insisted on digging
deep to do our best. Friends would applaud our love.
Support came when the children were born. Those
who were significant and mattered to us cheered us
on, in and through a career. And then came our personal and purposeful pursuits. Getting in shape, losing weight, learning new skills, getting divorced, remarried or starting from scratch. Or as we all share:
real estate investing.
At REIN the monthly meetings, online community and
faculty at head office are insistent reminders of The
Four Motivational Factors for your personal
growth and realization of any aim – real estate investment success included. When reviewing the four motivational factors, take on the exercise and selfevaluate;

THE BEAST DEVOURED R2O HOMES

Many of my rent-toowns are affected by
the fire called "The
Beast" which continues to burn in the Fort McMurray region. Some
of the homes are damaged, and others are
completely burned down.
There is a part of the rent-to-own agreement
which states:
9. Damage by Fire: In the event that the building(s) is damaged by fire and through no fault
of the Tenant and cannot be restored within a
reasonable time in the opinion of the Landlord,
this agreement shall terminate with no further
liability of either party.
What does this mean for a rent-to-own deal
and how are the tenant-buyers affected?
It means that in the event of a fire which is not
the tenants fault, that the deal can stop. Tenant
-buyers are returned their "option to purchase"
funds (AKA their forced savings for their deposit to buy the home at the end of the rent-toown).

The con is that if several houses burn down, and
the rent-to-own operator suddenly has to refund
several deals worth of option to purchase money,
they could be in a cash crunch. If the funds were
spent to subsidize living expenses, then they are
not there to return to the tenant-buyer.
I do not teach my students to spend the upfront
deposit money. It's best not to count your chickens before they hatch. Keep the tenant-buyers
deposit money in a separate bank account. Once
the deal is completed (and money partners are
paid), only then should you spend your profits.
The other option is to put together another rent-toown deal and find another house for the tenantbuyer. OR You if your tenant-buyer has good
enough credit at this point to get a mortgage and
enough savings for a down payment, send them
to your friendly realtor and the tenant-buyer can
do a normal purchase.
#END#

This has a huge impact on the deal maker, and
depending on how they run their business, it
can result in them going out of business or
ending up in court.
Many of the rent-to-own courses that I have
taken use headlines in their marketing saying
that you can make $20,000 up front or even
more. What is really going on is that the rent-to
-own business owner is putting the tenantbuyer's deposit money into their own account,
and using it to live off of.
The pro to doing this is you would not have to
wait 2 or even 3 years to start spending profits.
The real estate lawyer is able to do a creative
close where equity in the home is used for the
tenant-buyer (soon to be home-owner) to buy
the home. Normally, taking profits early would
not have a significant effect on the deal.... except in the event of a fire.

Repair Vs. Replace
As an investor you are always wanting to make sure
you maximize your cash flow and no one will watch
your pennies like you. When you have a property management company taking care of your properties they
will most likely look at the fastest and easiest fix possible, which often is not the most economical for you.
Appliances are a perfect example because I don’t know
how many times we have replaced refrigerators less
than ten years old simply because it is easier just to
order a new fridge. Now I do need to be fair because
refrigerators are almost a disposable item these days
but still it drives me crazy and I think you get my point.
General maintenance items like appliance repairs,
screens, broken doors, etc. are something you can’t
avoid and unless you are prepared to do the work yourself then you will have to bite the bullet and pay someone else. , Just make sure you are only paying for what
truly needs to be done.
When it comes to larger capital items such as windows,
roofing, kitchen make-over, bath room refitting, and
such, there are definitely ways to help reduce costs.
With bathrooms and kitchens there are “Recycle “
stores in most major markets where you can often pick
up materials/products for a fraction of the cost so I
would suggest you find a store like this and make a
habit of visiting it on a regular basis. With items like
windows and roofing you don’t want to cheap out here;
however, there is a tax saving strategy to consider.
When you incur an operating expense (such as a repair) then 100% of the cost is applied to the taxable
income, which seems logical. However, when you take
on a major repair such as re-shingling the entire roof
then 100% of the expense will reduce your cash flow
immediately but you can only use a small portion of the
expense to reduce your taxable income because it
must be depreciated over the life of the roof. As an example, if the new roof cost you $10,000 then that
means your cash flow has been reduced by the full
$10,000 yet you may only claim a tax deduction of 10%
($1,000) during that year. So you pay tax on the remaining $9,000 even though you don’t have the cash.
Not great for the cash flow during the year you replaced the roof for sure.
One way to circumvent this would be to call this major
capital expense a maintenance item; however, the Tax
man will not agree and your accountant will not recommend it (nor do I). Another way to avoid incurring it as a
capital expense is to do it as a maintenance item by
doing a portion of the roof this year, another portion
next year, and spread it out over 3-4 years. Now it becomes a legitimate maintenance expense. Windows
are another way to transfer a capital expense to a
maintenance expense. Instead of doing all 20 windows
at the same time you do 4 windows per year and ex-

pense them instead of capitalise them. Talk to your accountant for other tax saving
strategies☺.
The last thing I want to touch
on is capital repairs and
grandfathering. In many older properties they were
likely built in violation of today’s building codes and
as long as they remain “as-is” there is no issue or
requirement to update them because they are grandfathered. The challenge is that if you make major
changes then the municipality may force you to upgrade to current standards which can become very
costly. I am currently looking at a situation like this
because I have enclosed porches with stairs at the
back of one of my properties which definitely do not
follow today’s rules with respect to set back distances from the neighbouring lot. If I remove the porches
and replace them just with decks then they will want
me to follow the updated rules for setbacks and I
don’t have the space which means I can’t build my
new decks. So what can I do?
We will technically repair the existing porches by
building the new decks in and around the existing
porches. This will require more work, however; it will
allow me to remain grandfathered with respect to the
setbacks. Fun and games but it is definitely better to
know this ahead of time so you can work with it and
avoid a nasty surprise.

What You See Is What You Get
Recently I noticed an HSBC ad with
an inspirational message that said,
“Visionaries see the world differently.” What struck me in that moment
was that every one of us sees the
world differently - it is just that visionaries see a world
full of possibilities and opportunities. Others see a
world of danger, obligation and limitation. And what
we see is what we get.
Every result and experience we have in life is determined by how we see the world. Yet 99% of the time
we forget two important facts: 1) The results we create and our experience of life are completely determined by how we look at things; and 2) We can
choose how we look at things.
Here’s an example:
A few years ago I was leading a mastermind session
in Sylvan Lake, Alberta with some small business clients. Three of us were walking together beside the
lake and we came upon a large lot with a for sale sign
on it. I said to the others, “Wow. This would be an
awesome place to build a dream home!” Another fellow who is a draftsman said, “This is a great lot. You
could build an eight unit condo here.” The third member of our group responded, “Why waste a location
like this on a small condo? I can see building a highrise here!”
I was immediately struck with the significance of what
had just happened. My mind was limited to the narrow
possibility of a beautiful home. That was as far as I
could see. Therefore if I owned the lot, that’s all I
could create. The others saw bigger possibilities and
therefore more was possible for them. None of us
were wrong about what we saw, it’s just that my vision
was more limited.
Think about the goals you’ve accomplished over the
years and you’ll realize that you have rarely achieved
a goal if you weren’t able to visualize the end result
beforehand. You may WANT to play a big game, but if
you can’t both see and feel the end result in advance,

Affordability Is A State Of Mind
United we stand. Divided we fall. I’m sure we
have all heard this call for solidarity. But for
some reason, it rings hollow for first-time home
buyers trying to get into the hot real estate market. There is an easy solution to buying a house
in the crazy Vancouver market today (or any
crazy housing market), but my idea is rejected
by entry-level home buyers. They aren’t willing
to compromise their standards in order to get a
piece of the action, while they stand by helplessly and hopelessly watching the over-heated market price them out of the home buyers’ market.
My solution is joint-ownership where everyone
pools their money for the down payment and
either (a) jointly live in the house or (b) one of
the new joint-owners live onsite while the others
remain offsite investors. And I am not referring
to the bank of mom and dad. They’ve paid their
dues so no dipping into their bank account.

Canadians have the
same opportunity, if
not more, to build and
create wealth. Immigrants arrive with little
or no credit, unfamiliar with the language and customs, yet they have managed to build incredible
wealth through collaboration.
It’s time to re-think your priorities. You can have
sole ownership … one day. But in order to build
equity, you need to get into real estate. Even if it’s
just a fractional ownership, it’s better than having
100% of nothing. In my world, affordability is a
state of mind. It’s a matter of how much ownership
you are willing to share.

well and Canadians who are born and bred in
this country defend their right to sole ownership?

Canadian right to own their own house and not

Yuck, I could never stand having a tenant living
in the basement, that’s creepy. No way am I going to share the equity in my house with someone I hardly know. I can barely get along with
my family let alone roommates. I can’t move to a
strange neighbourhood that’s an hour away,
that’s not an option.
Immigrants have to be resourceful when they
come to a new country. They pool their resources. They make short term sacrifices for a
long term game plan. They are all about collaboration, contribution and cooperation for the good
of the whole team. They view each other as a
community first, individuals second.
The Chinese and East Indian community came
to Canada as labourers. They used to drive
economy cars like Toyota Corolla and Ford Focus. The next generation is driving BMW and
Mercedes.

Commercial Mortgages. What You Need
To Know!
The majority of lenders will consider 4
units and above a commercial deal and
fall into these credit rules
and policies. A smaller residential deal therefore would be
considered as a 4-plex building or smaller.
I get asked many times if I could do a brief over view of each
and the major differences that a person must know in order to
make the transition. I would say the major difference is that
80% of the bank’s focus will be on the property itself and the
net cash flow it produces. While on the small deals the bank
tends to focus 80% of its approval on you yourself and your
credit rating along with your income. For some people this is
a major bonus as they may have lots of investments around
but perhaps not a huge amount of net taxable income. Perhaps the Bigger Deal is for you?
The 80% focus in the apartment buildings would consist of
several items. 3 years’ worth of accountant prepared financial
statements, A building condition report also known as an inspection report done by an engineering firm usually and an
appraisal report. Lastly a current copy of tenants and leases
need to be provided along with an environmental assessment
of the land and area the building is sitting on. The 20% is
really your personal net worth statement with back up verification of all items, along with a copy of a recent credit bureau
and verification of some liquid assets. What are those and
why do they need to see that you ask? It is really showing the
lender you and or your partners have enough assets that you
could have access to in case of emergencies etc. I always
use the example that your new purchase for whatever reason
may require a $40,000.00 new roof shortly. Do you and your
partners
have some cash set aside for just in case items? (That is
really what they are looking for.) Obviously the older the
building the more money a lender will want to see set aside.
It is important to know and understand that conventional noninsured mortgage are available for large deals as well a person can get an insured high ratio mortgage for their multifamily building as well.
The CMHC insured mortgages typically have a lower interest
rate than the conventional mortgages do. Why is that? Simply
put it’s because the government of Canada is guaranteeing
the funds on the loan to the money lender. Because the risk
is much lower the lender is willing to accept a lower yield and
that savings is passed along to the consumer. Ok if the rates
are considerably lower than conventional mortgages then
why doesn’t everyone get a CMHC commercial mortgage for
their building? I am glad you asked… If any of you thought a
bank was conservative when they looked at the income and
expenses declared by a seller? How do you think the government who are putting tax payer’s money on
the line will review those same figures? Much stricter is all I
can say to that one. What I am finding in
today’s market is that the loan amounts that we are seeing
are relatively similar. So the advantage a person would receive is that their monthly mortgage payment would be smaller if they were to take out

a CMHC insured commercial mortgage. Now having said
that what the additional costs to consider are
here? Well you would have an insurance fee added on top
of your mortgage balance once the deal closes. 4.5% of
the loan amount is the typical insurance fee. There is also
an application fee associated with sending a deal over to
CMHC also. One school of thought is to save all of those

cashflow.
Others will tell you that over a 5 year term the savings

to do the math using both scenarios in order
to make the informed decision. Market conditions impact
these two calculations as well. Always talk to
your favorite Mortgage Broker to find out the best course
of action here.
Timing on getting your financing done is a key knowledge
point as well. Normally I recommend 60 days to get your
financing approved at the bank and another 30 days to
have the law office complete your transaction. If you decide to use CMHC to insure your new mortgage you
should be looking to add 3 to 4 weeks onto your timelines
just because of the extra level of scrutiny that you are
adding to the process. Don’t forget that you need the lender to say YES and then you need to have your deal sent
off to have CMHC say YES as well. These are market
timeliness and some folks may even have a shorter turn
around on this process but that would be the exception to
the rule in my opinion. Going with less time could put undue stress on your team and that is not good for future
business.

Self Management vs. Hiring a Property Manager

When dealing with rental
properties, it’s inevitable
that things will go wrong
from time to time. From
unexpected repairs, tenants move-outs before the lease ends, property damage, the list goes on and on. Most of the problems
have relatively simple solutions but only if there is a
properly executed system in place to address these
issues as they arise; this is where property management comes into play. The key to any successful property management company comes down to 4 key deliverables:
• How fast they’re able to resolve problems
• How effective they are at keeping costs down
• How quickly they can turn-over tenants (fill vacancies)
• Their ability to communicate progress or failures
along the way
If you’re brand new to investing in rental properties, I
would highly recommend you learn by overseeing as
much of the day-to- day responsibilities as possible.
It’s important to get an understanding of what is going
on with your tenants and the property before handing
it over to a management company. This way you can
better understand the cost, time, and challenges facing possible 3rd party management. If you decide it’s
best to hire a property manager, it’s important to recognize that there are many different levels of service
out there, and you’ll need to ask the right type of questions to get the service that fits your needs.
Questions like:
• How long has the company been in business and
how many units are they currently managing?
• What is their success ratio with filling vacancies?
• Do they have in-house contractors, plumbers, and
electricians or sub everything out?
• What fees do they charge if a suite remains vacant?
• What services do they offer to encourage tenant
lease renewals?
• How do they communicate with their property owners?

The other option is to continue to manage the properties
either yourself or hire and train your own staff. Selfmanagement offers you more controls and in most cases better overall service to your tenants and your property simply because you’ll only be caring for your own
portfolio. Keep in mind, that although you will be retaining more cash flow from the properties due to not having
to pay property management fee’s, it’s considerably
more time spend dealing with your investments.
For myself, I’ve tried both with different levels of success. The hardest part I’ve found with using a property
management company is getting them to fill vacancies
quickly. They are managing multiple properties and will
only have so many applicants apply for their rental units.
In certain situations, this can become very frustrating
because essentially you end up paying a property management company a monthly fee that results in more
vacancies than you had before. Even a one-month vacancy can cost $1000+. This can start to hurt your properties cash-flow if not monitored closely.
Today I manage my portfolio with a small staff that I
have self trained. I wouldn’t advise or recommend this to
someone with less than ten properties, or that hasn’t
managed their own units for at least a few years. It takes
a lot of effort to train your people and put the systems in
place but for me, it’s been the right choice. We’re always
focusing on new ways to keep costs down while aiming
to deliver an exceptional customer experience to our
tenants. The result has been minimum tenant turnover
while keeping operating costs in check against our projected monthly/quarterly/yearly budgets. Furthermore,
when vacancies do arise, since I’m only looking after my
own portfolio I can put all or energy into filling the unit(s)
quickly as possible. This has saved me thousands of
dollars in lost rent, especially due to current economic
conditions here in Edmonton.
Ultimately it’s up to you to decide what’s best for you
based on your allowed time, skill level, investor commitments, and risk tolerance. Whichever you choose, remember that property management is critical to success
in real estate investing. Those who make property management a priority and are continuously looking for ways
to improve, stand the best chance for long-term success.

The Pain Of Being Persistent
(The Self Delusion Factor).

Sometimes its difficult to know when to quit doing
something that doesn’t seem to be working, and
when to plow on and be persistent in spite of the inevitable set backs you face.

sented, does it still make sense?”.

We all hear from super successful people that tell
their stories of persisting through seemingly insurmountable obstacles in order to reach their dreams.

Once I’ve started and I’m in the thick of it and I’m not
seeing even my ‘worst case’ scenario panning out, then
I re-evaluate things.

And on the other hand, we also hear things like “A
foolish consistency is the hobgoblin of simple
minds” (which means that if we keep plowing ahead
the same way, in spite of evidence to the contrary,
that isn’t very smart).

I ask myself...

It’s no wonder we find ourselves questioning why it is
taking us so long to reach our goals even though we
are persisting.
I think for me it comes down to the ‘Self Delusion
Factor’.
If I’m interested in getting into something new, I want
to see if it is truly viable or not. Opportunities are
usually presented in the best possible light, and we
are shown shiny ‘best case scenarios’.
After determining that it truly is a legitimate opportunity (other real people have had success… not just the
person who is pitching it), I like to put it through the
following acid test.
I ask myself “If this takes twice as long, costs twice
as much, and is only ½ as successful as it is pre-

If my answer is ‘yes’, then I go for it. If the answer is
‘no’… then I don’t.

“Am I truly giving this new thing my full attention? Have
I studied the materials thoroughly? Have I done suggested assignments and tasks? Have I used any support that is available? Have I taken action and actually
implemented what I’ve been learning?”
If the answer is “No” or “Not really”, then I don’t blame
the opportunity or the program I’m taking, I look inward
and try to figure out why I’m not really serious about it.
More often than not, it’s because I’m too firmly entrenched in my comfort zone, and I don’t feel enough
pain to get out of it.
Then I either crank up the ‘pain’ (aka motivation), or I
decide to consciously stop doing this ‘new’ thing. I accept that it isn’t what I’m truly interested in at the moment, and I either go back to what I was doing before,
or look for something that is in closer alignment with
what I truly want.
