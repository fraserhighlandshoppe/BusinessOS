BALANCING ACT with Randall Orser

When Should You Change Course?

Plans are important for an entrepreneur. They serve as a guide and allow you to focus your efforts
in a direction you have determined as profitable or beneficial for your start-up. But sometimes
things change. Your target demographic may not be what you thought it was. Your product may not
work in the field as well as you thought. Whatever the situation, you'll have to ask yourself whether
you should stay the course or start pivoting.
Changing your strategy takes a lot of time and makes all your previous effort meaningless. A weak strategy coupled with
consistency will almost always do better than multiple intelligent strategies that never reach fruition. On the other hand,
forcing a broken strategy because that's what you said you would do can lead to failure. The trick is figuring out when to
change course and when to stick to your guns.
1.

Look at Measurable Metrics

Big dreams and concepts are great. As an entrepreneur, you'll need those dreams and concepts to drive you forward, to make
your start-up mean something more than a money-making machine. However, those dreams and concepts should not
determine whether or not you change your plans. You can't measure them, so you cannot use them to determine whether or
not you're approaching your goals.
When deciding on your start-up's direction, think about a number of key metrics, such as your current cash flow, sales
numbers, your prospective clients; if you can count it, you can use it to judge your company's current state. Use those
numbers to figure out what to do, and convince your partners and investors that it's the right thing to do.
2.

Update Your Assumptions

You're going to make a lot of educated guesses throughout your life as an entrepreneur. You can gather all the information you
want, but ultimately all your predictions are just that -- predictions. Things can and will change, and you need to adjust your
assumptions accordingly.
Begin your regular meetings by looking over your assumptions and discussing whether or not they till hold water. Use those
discussions to figure out how your plans are going.
3.

Keep Track of Your Progress

Set a regular meeting with the managers of your company to track your start-up's progress. Each meeting has the singular
purpose of determining if your plan is succeeding. Start by taking a look at what you expect to happen and compare it to
what's actually happening. Talk about measurable things: are the numbers what the plan expects them to be? Should you
adjust your plan to compensate for any setbacks? Were those setbacks due to a flaw in the plan or to unrelated complications?
Adjust your plans accordingly.
Make sure that all relevant team members are informed ahead of time when these meetings will occur. Keep them short and to
the point.
4.

Examine Your Execution

One of the best reasons to consider changing your plans involves execution. The best plans won't work if you can't execute
them well. You may not be able to pull it off for many reasons, from lack of capital to not having the right people in your
team. If your execution failed, can you fix the cause of the failure? Can you afford to hire the right people to get your
marketing campaign off the ground? Can you get the capital to expand your operations? If there is no feasible way to make
your plans work, given your context, you may have to change your plans.
