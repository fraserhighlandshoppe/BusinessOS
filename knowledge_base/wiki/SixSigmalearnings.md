Since I will be sharing "Lean" Six Sigma, I will try this post to be "lean" as possible. Going on a 3 day training for Yellow Belt Certification is such a blast. Imagine a 200 page manual squeezed in 3 8 hour-sessions? Good thing our Black Belt trainer knows his craft big time. Yes, of course he must but I'm just comparing him to other trainers who are so "bookish" that at the end of a training, I don't know what I've been through. Anyway, to cut this short, the 200 page manual was squeezed perfectly.

Every little detail is critical. I can't afford to miss a page as I know I will be missing something big. But of all I've learned, here's my top 5.

===Create your "3 Ps" - Policy, Process, Procedure===

Right after the training, I started reviewing my group's Business Processes. All along, I know everthing is going smooth. I have rules, my people follow them, I score them and my vendors, and so on. But no, I know there's still a lot of digging needed. Just because everything seems to be going smoothly, that's it.

If I ask you this question, will you answer Yes?

"If you hand over your Business Process document to a new team member, will he be able to grasp it even without further instructions?

That's one thing I need to do now. My BPs should be a helpful manual. That if someone new sees it, he can easily follow through. This is the essence of a Policy, Process, and Procedure that is Lean Six Sigma based. It is well written that even a person who isn't familiar with what you are doing will understand. It has to be very specific and crafted in an organized manner.

===Eliminating Non-Valuable Things===

I had a session with my co-supervisors. My manager tasked me to summarize what I've learned from the training and share it with the rest of the supervisors. Of all the items I discussed, we are focusing on eliminating the non-valuable tasks we do. For the coming weeks before the end of the year, our target is to zero out our NV list.

Doing this will make room for other valuable but taken for granted tasks. We all must admit that in some way or another, our TO DO list at the beginning of the year is a mixture of valuable and non-valuable things. The bad thing is when we prioritize, we take the real valuable ones for granted .

There's a science on how to do this and that's what I will be working on. I will act as a consultant to the other supervisors and help them zero out the NV list. It's gonna be a win-win situation because we will also be able to target decreasing overtime and who knows, we might not need additional manpower and realize that what we have now is already enough to survive day to day work.

===Quick Wins should be Easily Reversible===

Quick Wins. I love this.

Even if you are at the middle of a DMAIC process, it's important that you know how to identify your Quick Wins. Quick Wins are solutions that may come along while you are in the middle of an analysis. But not because it solved one thing, it is already a Quick Win. Consider it Quick Win only if it can be implemented easily, cheap to implement, can be implemented within the group, and most of all Easily Reversible.

My first Lean Six Sigma session with my team was when we analyzed our Headcount and Access Management Policy. Policies, processes, and procedures were revisited. We were able to identify a run down of issues but it's good to note that our Quick Wins saturated the list. We quickly implemented them. It's such a motivating moment knowing that despite of the issues we need to solve, there are quick wins to balance everything.

===Let DATA do the Talking===

Need I say more?

I've shared this from my recent post. If only data can shout, they will and they will tell you what needs to be done. Sometimes, you need not deeper analysis. Your scorecard tells you what needs to be improved, what needs to me maintained.

There was a time when one of our accounts has a failing service level. The immediate solution created by my team was to augment the manpower. Tap agents from other accounts, skill them with the skills of the account that is failing and help in salvaging service level. This is a good solution but only band aid; not long term.

What my team forgot to do is to study the root cause of the problem. When I studied the data, all else are good except for the agents' failure to meet the target handling time. That's it. The data is shouting and telling that I need to do something with the agents' behavior. The initial solution identified is just covering the hole in the ball instead of replacing the ball.

===It's not a Methodology...it's a Culture===

Nothing is more wonderful than everybody embracing the principle of Lean Six Sigma. If only I will be the one to apply it in the whole organization, I don't think I'll accomplish a thing. Lean Six Sigma is not a set fo rules to follow nor helpful tips to apply. It is a culture. It's like using a soap when you take a bath, brushing your teeth after every meal, and eating on a plate using a spoon and a fork.

For it to become a culture, it must be practiced day in day out, every minute, every second, every thought, and every action.

Do I sound so idealistic? Yes, maybe. But this is how it should be. I guess nothing else matter than believing in these ideas and turning them into action.
