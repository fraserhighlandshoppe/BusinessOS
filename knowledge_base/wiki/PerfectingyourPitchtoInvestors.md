==Perfecting Your Pitch to Investors==

This spring, I found myself preparing for a similar situation, only this time it was in advance of my CBC Television Dragons’ Den taping (the show airs in October). I wanted to share five things I have learned throughout my journey on how to effectively pitch investors.

1. Elevate your elevator pitch. Every entrepreneur needs an elevator pitch; a captivating, 30-second opening where you concisely articulate what your company has to offer. In the beginning, I spent too much time talking about my initial product line – leak-resistant women’s underwear – when what investors really wanted to hear about was my long-term vision for the company and how we planned to transform the intimate apparel market. I like the example of how Uber’s Travis Kalanick described his business not just as as an app that links available drivers with riders, but also as a next-generation global transportation company that is “evolving the way the world moves.” Find a way to seamlessly transition between what you are doing today and the long-term vision you have for your company.

2. Nail your numbers. If you don’t know your numbers, you might as well not show up – and I’m not just referring to revenue. One of the catchphrases that will forever be ingrained in me from business school is, “if you don’t understand the costs, you don’t understand the business.” Different business models involve different metrics, so be sure to know the ones that pertain to you. For product-driven businesses, make sure you know your gross margin, cost of goods sold, cost of customer acquisition, monthly burn rate (monthly spend), as well as any economies of scale that you may enjoy at a later date.

3. Know your market (and your competitors). Understanding your market also means understanding your competition, and saying you do not have any is not an option. Even if no one is doing exactly the same thing that you are, you are still competing for your end customer’s share of wallet and share of time. On that note, startups and small companies typically have limited resources, both financially and when it comes to personnel. You need to have a clear picture of who your end consumer is and a cost-effective, realistic explanation of how you are going to reach them.

4. Do your homework. I like to think of investor pitches like a first date, only where the ideal outcome is a proposal. Taking on investors is a major, long-term commitment and picking the right partner who will be there in sickness and in health is essential. Figure out who you’re pitching to. What does their typical investment look like – in terms of size, stage of the business and industry? Who’s in their current portfolio? What is their professional background and area of expertise? This research will give you a good indication as to whether there is a fit, as well as prepare for you for questions that they might ask.

5. Be yourself. Almost every angel investor and venture capitalist that I’ve met says that they invest in people first and ideas second. With that in mind, be yourself. Granted, a polished, articulate and great version of yourself – but yourself nonetheless. Let your personality and your passion for your business shine through.

 No matter what you are selling, make the other party feel at ease. If I can get an all-male panel of potential investors passionate about the prospect of functional, leak-proof women’s underwear, then anything is possible. To be successful, you will need to eat, sleep and breathe your idea, and be able to convince potential investors that you’re the best person to bring that vision to life.

==How to get Investors to sign on the dotted line==
 http://www.theglobeandmail.com/report-on-business/small-business/sb-tools/top-tens/how-to-get-investors-to-sign-on-the-dotted-line/article20961313/


A recent survey of more than 500 Canadian small business owners from business management solutions firm, Sage North America found that more than half went from business idea to launch in less than six months. The survey also found 70 per cent had their first sale within six months of launching.

What can you do to secure funding for your business? Here are 10 tips to remember:

1. Invest in yourself. Self-funding is an option every business owner should consider, even if the ultimate goal is to attract outside funding. No one expects you to invest your life savings into your startup, but if you’re not willing to invest your own money into your business, don’t expect anyone else to do it.

2. Find your funding ‘fit.’ The road that travels from ‘great idea’ to ‘great returns’ can sometimes be a long and winding one, and not every entrepreneur will see an immediate return on investment. If you’re relying on someone else, it’s crucial to pursue funding sources that fit your values, feel comfortable and allow you to stay committed to the vision of your business.

3. Family and friends can be a vote of confidence. Approaching family and friends for funding before going to banks, angel investors or venture capitalists is never a bad idea – assuming they believe in the idea and share your values. Most investors love entrepreneurs who have been backed by the people who know them best. In fact, if you don’t have family and friends supporting your business, it could raise a red flag with potential investors.

4. Don’t take a big salary. This one might seem like a no brainer, but it’s advice some entrepreneurs fail to heed. A big salary, especially early on, can lead to big trouble. Whether you plan to borrow from family and friends or a venture-capital firm, giving yourself a big salary will raise eyebrows.

5. Never go dark. I’ve yet to meet a successful entrepreneur who didn’t experience setbacks during their early growth stage. Taking the plunge and building a business from scratch may be exhilarting, but it can also be challenging, confusing and stressful.

Even during the toughest of times, it’s imperative to keep the lines of communication with your investors open. It’s better to be honest about your setbacks than to leave investors hanging.

Regular updates to your backers can also be beneficial, as it gives investors an opportunity to provide feedback and advice, which is especially important when you’re in the weeds every day and need to look at the big picture.

6. Operate like a big business. Given how rapidly Canadian entrepreneurs are moving from idea to selling their products and services to real customers, preparing for growth starts well before launch. Businesses that think and act like bigger, structured business are in a better position further their growth efforts, stay disciplined and establish themselves as an attractive candidate for funding at an early stage.

Investors are looking for entrepreneurs who are making data-driven decisions wherever possible. Small business owners who regularly keep track of their company’s financial health and customer interactions through the use of online invoicing and accounting software will be more attractive than those who manage their books with spreadsheets or a shoebox full of receipts.

7. Crowdfunding is here to stay. Once viewed a quirky way to fuel off-beat projects, crowdfunding is now a respectable, legitimate and powerful source of funding. Crowdfunding is a quick, low-barrier option if you are looking to get around the traditional, stand-in-line venture capital models. It can also help entrepreneurs raise the profile for their business idea and provide real-world feedback from online investors.

8. Timing is key when pitching angel investors. With less than five per cent of all businesses receiving funding from angel investors, competition is fierce and timing is everything. Unlike crowdfunding, angel investors can bring expertise and resources to your business and can guide you in the all-important early years.

It’s advisable to approach angels at the very early stages of your business. Typically, angels are more likely to see the potential in a wider range of businesses, even if they don’t have any direct experience in a specific market. They usually view unproven businesses with great imagination and optimism than venture capitalist, so a high-potential idea could resonate.

9. Wait at least a year before approaching the venture capitalists. Unlike many angel investors, entrepreneurs looking to tap into venture capital need to prove their business is financially viable and have worked to minimize risk. Venture capitalists will be looking for businesses aimed at a hot sector with a strong management team and a functioning product or proven customer base.

This route is only advisable for entrepreneurs who are willing to wait at least a year to qualify for funding.

10. Numbers always matter. As a co-founder of Round 13 Capital and an investor myself, I can tell you numbers always matter, even when looking at an early startup. Investors want to know that you have tested your existing and potential customer base and are able to say, “I can get a lot of them for approximately this cost and I have a very strong sense that over time, they’re going to be worth this much to me.”

You need to be able to show that what your customer base’s worth is significantly larger compared to its cost to prove to investors of any sort that you can build a business around it.

The good news is that there hasn’t been a better time to start a new business than now. Right now, startup funding from incubators and organized angel groups in Canada is quite advanced. So, that $100,000, $200,000 or $500,000 cheque is a lot easier to get today than it was a short time ago.
