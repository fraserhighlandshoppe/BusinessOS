Ted owns a policy on the life of his wife Laura and has named himself as the beneficiary. Laura dies and Ted receives the benefit from the policy. Unfortunately, Laura had a lot of creditors and Ted is surprised to receive letters from creditors demanding him to clear the debts using the proceeds of the life insurance policy. Are the life insurance proceeds protected from the creditors?

a) Yes, because Ted is the spouse of Laura and therefore is a preferred beneficiary.

b) No, because Ted is the policy owner and therefore does not fall under the restricted definition of beneficiary.

c) Yes, because the policy was owned by Ted and not Laura.

d) No, because life insurance proceeds are always protected from creditors.


Rationale: It all comes down to the fact that Ted is BOTH the policy owner and the beneficiary. This means that Ted owns the policy on his wife, Laura, (who is the life insured) and Ted, as the beneficiary, will also receive the death benefit when Laura dies.

Under provincial insurance legislation, if you are the policy owner and the beneficiary you do not qualify as the legal definition of beneficiary and thus there is no creditor protection. Proceeds would not be protected from the claims of the life insured’s creditors if the owner is the beneficiary. Therefore, the death benefit is NOT creditor protected because Ted is BOTH the policy owner and the beneficiary.

To fix this problem, (i.e., to make the death benefit creditor protected), Laura needs to be the policy owner and life insured while making her husband ONLY the beneficiary.

Note that nowadays, the life insured is also usually the policy owner (i.e., you take out and own the policy on your own life). There is also a difference between the legal definition of beneficiary and the term beneficiary used every day. The term “beneficiary,” as contained in the provincial insurance legislation, has a restricted meaning for some legal purposes.
