==Insurance==
*Policy Holder - person who controls the policy.  They make the premiums, they make the decisions, they name the beneficiary.
*Beneficiary - person who gets the payout
*Life Insured (a.k.a annuitant) - person on whose death the policy is covering.
*Single life - insurance on 1 person's life
*Joint first to die - coverage on 2 or more people, and pays out upon the first of the group dying.  Typicaly used when 2 or more share a debt obligation.  Also typically used for business buy-sell agreements.
*Joint last to die - covering 2 or more people, and pays out once the last is deceased.  Used where a risk doesn't occur until the last passes (e.g. taxes on married spouses capital gains)
*Death benefits can have an increasing amount, level amount or decreasing amount.  The level amount is typically sold.
**Increasing term are becoming difficult to purchase in Canada.  Riders are replacing/replicating this.
===How premiums are set===
*Non renewable - at the end of the term you have to reapply for insurance.  Changes in health could result in denied.
*Renewable - guaranteed able to renew.  New premiums based upon age.
*re-entry - essentially renewable, but has different premium schedules.
**lower premiums up front so can be good if only needed for 1 or 2 terms.
*Convertable - lets person convert to a permanent life insurance.
**Higher premiums because greater longer term risk assumed by insurance company.
**Conversions are treated as extensions with respect to certain legal contract provisions (i.e. suicide, contestibility period)
====Term Insurance====
Mortality cost (face value + company expenses including profit) * probability of death
====Permanent====
Premiums are typically level for the duration of the insured life, so the premiums at the start are much higher as they are building up a reserve against what the future premiums would have become.

A similar pricing to Term is in effect - face value + the sum of long term overhead costs (marketing, commissions, admin, etc) - investment returns divided by the expected number of years of premiums.  The insurance company will use conservative numbers because the policy could last 50-60 years.

*Participating policy - if the insurance company has much better investment returns than projected, some of the excess is returned to policy holders.  This is termed a dividend, but is taxed differently than corporate dividends.  Ways the dividends can be paid:
**Cash
**Premium reduction
**Accumulation (deposited into a side account and invested)
**Paid up additions
***An additional (usually separate) insurance policy with CSV is purchased using the dividend
**Term insurance (1 year)
**Adjusted death benefits/CSV
*Non participating - excess investment returns remain pure profit for the company.  For this reason the premiums are typically lower than participating.
===Premium Options===
*Ongoing (paid annually, quarterly or monthly.  Actual payments for sub-annual are adjusted to account for lost investment income)
*Single - a lump sum that "pays-up" the policy.
*Limited - premiums are paid every X years.
===Benefit Options===
*Guaranteed - premiums and payout guaranteed
*Adjustable - premiums and payout are reviewed every X years and adjusted based upon the insurance companies actual experience versus expected.  NO LONGER COMMON IN CANADA.

===Death===
Insurance companies use 2 statistics:
*Life Expectancy 
**A male's life expectancy is 80 years of age until they reach 60, where it goes to 85.  Once they hit 80, the expectancy hits 90, and at 90, 102.
*Probability of death
**A male's probability of dying that particular year is less than 1/10 of a percent until 40. At 50 it is 2/10%.  Between 60 and 65 it goes above 1%.
Females have similar risk curves, but their risk of death is lower than males.

===Risk Management===
Strategies:
*Avoidance
**By not putting oneself into a situation, you can avoid a risk.
*Reduction
**You can take steps to minimize the impact, or minimize the risk (odds of happening) of an event.
*Retention
**Accepting that bad things happen.  This is most acceptable for low risk or low impact events.
*Transfer
**Insurance is a form of risk transfer
Most people have the wrong type of insurance or have insured the wrong things.  

You insure assets, not liabilities.  

Make sure you buy insurance on your life to protect your dependents.  However, if you are dependent on someone else (i.e. business partner who owns half your business) you might want to buy insurance on them to cover buying back their half.  Another example is if you have a real estate empire and you die, there will be a tax liability (capital gains of the properties) that will be due.  Without enough liquid assets (cash) a property may need to be sold at a low in the market.  Carrying sufficient insurance to cover the tax liability may be worthwhile.  In this case you are insuring an asset to cover the liability it will generate.

Buy non-participatory policies.  Participatory policies is where the company pays you a ''dividend'' each year.  In truth, they overcharge their customers and then pay back some of that overpayment to the policy holders.  What this does in fact is pass some of the insurance risk to the policy holders.  And since the purpose of insurance is to hand the risk to someone else, you are paying the insurer to take on the risk, some of which you are now taking back.

====Insurance Types====

Having the right insurance is necessary to protect the financial future of your dependents.  

The purpose of life insurance is to ensure that your living estate (assets minus liabilities) plus the insurance provide enough to wind down your affairs and provide your '''dependents''' the desired standard of living.  Think of life insurance as ''income replacement insurance'' or ''financial protection for dependents''.  If you don't have dependents, your don't need life insurance.  If you have accumulated sufficient wealth to protect your dependents, you don't need life insurance.

Calculating the coverage required:
*Lump sum costs (i.e. funeral expenses)
*Pay off existing debts
**Creditors can chase after life insurance policy payouts.  Make sure to see [[Insurance Beneficiaries Creditor Protection]]!
*Replacement income
**The insurance company pays a lump sum.  How much is needed to be invested so that a 7% annual return rate provides the desired income level?
**Inflation.  Averages 4% over a long time
**How many years of replacement income are required?
**How much for the pay yourself first (or other buffer?)
*Future obligations such as college tuition (invested for X years, at 7%)
Insure a non working spouse to pay off the debt, funeral expense, and to cover net increases in expenses for child care, etc.

If both spouses are working, the calculation is more difficult.  Don't forget the case of if both spouses die at the same time.  You can get insurance to cover this particular case as well.

Don't insure children.  No matter how much you love them, they are a financial liability, not a financial asset.  (You can insure their final expenses though!)

===Permanent life versus term insurance===
*Term insurance is a temporary insurance - best to cover things that the liability goes away within the term.
**funding child care or education funds
**Paying off a mortgage or loan
*Permanent insurance is best for covering things where the risk doesn't go away.
**funeral expenses

Permanent insurance doesn't expire as long as the premiums are payed.  

Things to consider:
*Coverage
*Premiums
*Renewability
*Cash Surrender Value
*Investment 
*Benefits
====Term Insurance====
Term insurance pays out the face value upon the instance occuring.  It has a specific length of time (the term) for which it is valid.  Once the term ends, the insurance no longer exists.  There is no cash value, investment (savings) or other entailments. 

Make sure the policy is renewable (ensures future insurability).

Make sure there is a guaranteed maximum premium increase rate to ensure events don't make the cost of future renewals prohibitive (new medical conditions or company pricing the policy uncompetitively)

Make sure the policy is convertible.  This lets you convert to a whole/universal life plan once you hit 65 and can't buy term anymore.  However, by 65 you want to be self insured (more than enough assets to cover debts, future costs for dependents, etc).

====Whole Life====
Otherwise known as cash-value.  It has a cash surrender value.

This insurance vehicle buys the same insurance as the term insurance (at the same cost) and then invests the difference in the insurance companies saving vehicle.  Only difference is the insurance company is going to be less efficient (corporate overhead, commissions) in investing the difference.

As this is a forced savings, one potential benefit is there is saved money that can be used in later years to make up the difference between current insurance prices and future prices by tapping into this cash value (assuming the policy and broker allow this).

One downside to this type is that when you die, the insurance only pays out the face value.  If there are any of the savings in it, they are kept by the insurance company.

If the policy is canceled before death, the CSV may be received.
====Term 100====
Similar to Whole Life, but there is no cash surrender value, and the premiums stop at age 100.
====Universal Life====
In this type, it is like whole life in a savings vehicle plus insurance vehicle.  However, they are uncoupled, so the savings is paid to the beneficiaries as well as the face value.
 If you don't need insurance, the tax shelter won't generally work for you because of the cost of the insurance.
Universal life is more suited to people with substantial assets who want to protect their families from substantial taxes on their estate after their death.

*you can choose to pay much more than the cost of your insurance.
**Part of your premium goes into a reserve to cover the immediate and future cost of insuring you, 
**while the rest goes into a tax-sheltered fund to earn interest.
*You control the savings in the reserve account and you decide how to invest the money. You can stick with fixed-income investments or choose from a variety of mutual funds.
*When you die, the insurance company pays the death benefits and the money from the savings component to your survivors.
*Caveats:
**many companies charge high fees (surrender charge) if you want to get out of a universal life policy in the first 10 years.
**Administration fees tend to be high. 
**management fees on mutual funds held inside a universal life plan may be higher than if you held the same funds outside the policy.

Keep in mind: why you want to buy life insurance:
*to protect the well-being of your dependents? 
*create a savings plan 
*tax shelter?

The Universal Life policy acts as a permanent tax shelter.

There is a policy exemption that makes any gains under the specified level in the policy tax deferred.

*Quick Pay policies and Over-funded policies have lost some of their tax exempt benefits in 2016.

One way to extract the Investment portion of the policy is to have an insurable event (i.e. death).

==Segregated Funds==
In its most basic form, [[Segregated Funds]] are an investment with an added insurance policy protecting it. These insured guarantees are generally between 75% to 100% protection of the principal on both death and maturity. If you invest $150,000 into a given Segregated Fund and the market performs well, you keep the principal and the market gains. If the market tanks and the value dips below your initial investment of $150,000, your principal is still protected based on the guarantees you choose. This feature protects your investment from downhill markets and provides a safety net for your money. Historically, these investments have been much more conservative than the stock market, but they have since branched out to include a more varied series of managed funds to mirror the wide array of different mutual funds.
