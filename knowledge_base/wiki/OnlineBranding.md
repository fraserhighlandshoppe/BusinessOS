==Branding Yourself==

Nike is easily remembered for its famous tagline, “Just Do It,” and GEICO insurance for its amusing gecko mascot. What will make you memorable as a job seeker?  Defining and expressing your personal brand -- in much the same way as major corporations have a brand -- helps you stand out among the competition for jobs. 

Your own brand is made up of all the qualities that make you unique.  A brand is not exactly the same as an image -- image sometimes implies a superficial veneer that may be insincere, conjured up, or fleeting.  Instead, a brand is consistent and genuine; it's how you come across to others -- your real self -- time and time again. 

Branding from the Inside Out

What's My Brand? 

Living Your Brand
Branding from the Inside Out

Your brand is you on the inside and the outside, but especially on the inside. Sure, part of it comes from those outside qualities, such as how you carry yourself, what you typically wear, how you groom yourself.  A big part of your brand could be something distinctive about your look -- cool eye glasses, a classic hairstyle you've never changed because it's always in style and flattering, a closet full of colorful neckties.  But your brand is mostly you on the inside -- the more substantive qualities.  It's your experience, knowledge, talents, and skills, your special expertise, your unique way of looking at a situation and solving a problem.
What's My Brand?

Building your brand is relatively easy because you already have one.  You just might not realize it, and it might need some fine-tuning. But it's already in you.  The key is to ensure the branding you want to project is the one people actually see in you.  That takes attention, perspective, and the ability to recognize your unique characteristics – good and bad – and create a lasting impression that’s positive.  Here are some suggestions for defining your brand:

1. Determine what your brand might already be.  Ask friends, colleagues, clients, anyone who knows you well, what your most distinctive characteristics are.  What do they think makes up the essence of who you are? Listen for themes and overlap in what you hear.  Those might be clues to defining your brand.  Review prior performance reviews – what consistent strengths shine through or are singled out?

2. Think of how you want to be recognized.  What do you like about yourself?  Which of your talents, knowledge, or accomplishments are you most proud of?  What are your core competencies and your technical or business skills?  Consider your past job titles and functional expertise.  Are you viewed as an individual expert or leader of teams?

3. Think about people you admire and respect.  Why do you feel that way about them?  Your brand attributes have to be your own and not someone else's, but thinking of what you like in others might give you ideas of which qualities of your own you want to tout.

4. Examine your online identity.  Do you already have something of a brand established online?  Google yourself and see what comes up.  Are you visible on the Internet for particular experience, expertise, or involvements you've had?  These may be your hallmarks. 
Living Your Brand

Once you've established your brand, communicate it clearly and consistently in all that you do professionally. Use language that reflects your brand in your résumé and your job search business card, highlight your key brand attributes when networking and interviewing. In other words, live your brand.

Refining and communicating your brand will be an ongoing process throughout your career because your brand is enhanced with each new experience and skill you develop.  But you can define your personal brand fairly quickly and easily for your immediate job search purposes.




Creating Your Online Brand

Prospective employers and recruiters turn to the Internet not only to source candidates, but to learn more about candidates who have already applied for open positions. They monitor social media networking sites and also use search engines to research candidates. Google is seen by many as the most popular, so the term "googling" has entered our vernacular. Being googled in your job search is advantageous if you do have an online identity (also called a digital identity), meaning that a search yields plenty of references, or "hits," to you online. It's a good thing if all the information about you is positive, relevant, and reflective of your personal brand. (See Branding Yourself for more information on personal branding in general. The focus here is on your brand online.) 

Being googled is not such a good thing when a search brings up little to no information about you, or negative information. Managing your online brand involves developing positive online information that reflects well on your image and represents your most marketable talents. Having an intriguing and positive online identity is key to attracting recruiters and employers. There are several ways to do this - have a robust profile on sites like LinkedIn and Facebook, tweet on Twitter, reply to blog posts and networking discussions, and maintain a personal website. LinkedIn is especially useful for helping you maximize your Internet presence. Often, your LinkedIn profile will be the first result in a Google or Yahoo search of your name. (See the LinkedIn subtopic for more information).

Creating and managing your online brand involves both developing the positive information and image by which you want to be known in the market and guarding against negative or less-than-flattering information that people might associate with you online. To learn more about creating, managing, and monitoring your brand, read on.

Creating and Managing Your Online Brand
Cleaning Up Your Online Brand
Creating and Managing Your  Online Brand

Personal branding has risen to the forefront. Just as companies invest resources in developing brands and marketing collateral, you too should devote time to identifying your personal brand. Think about your resume, 30-second commercial, job search action plan, and business cards. All your messages and networking documents should be consistent and relevant. Use social media networking to thoughtfully communicate your brand to a broader audience via blog posts, online discussion groups, personal websites, tweets, and profiles. (Refer to these subtopics in the navigation pane on the left of this page.)

As a job seeker, you should maintain constant awareness of your cyberspace image. To keep an eye on where, why, and how you appear online, set up a Google alert to monitor your online identity. Each time your name shows up on the Internet, you’ll receive an email update of the new content. Also check out websites such as Pipl, and ZoomInfo to see what else might be lurking out there on the internet about you.

Even if you are new to social media networking and have yet to post your first profile, there will still likely be information about you. Information about conferences you've attended, presentations you've made, articles you've written, newspaper photos and mentions, and a wealth of other records are often now in digitized form and searchable by recruiters and others

Most of this information is usually positive and helpful, or at least neutral. But, occasionally job seekers find information that could be damaging or is just not consistent with their personal brand. This is referred to as "digital dirt."
Cleaning Up Your Online Brand

The term digital dirt describes negative information on the Internet that can damage reputations, cause embarrassment, prevent job seekers from getting hired, and even get people fired. It might include such things as photos of unprofessional behavior, public records or references to lawsuits, or evidence of a moonlighting business. And it might not even be about you! If you have a common name, you could be confused with someone else whose online brand is less than flattering to you.

Should you find a problem, there are three choices for cleaning up digital dirt to protect your online brand, according to the book Find a Job through Social Networking by Diane Crompton and Ellen Sautter, consultants with Right Management.  

    Wash it out - Get rid of it entirely by asking the hosts of the website to remove the information. But unless you or someone you know well created or posted the content in the first place, you might have a difficult time getting the owners of sites to remove the offending content.
    Wash over it - Create enough new online content about yourself that negative and irrelevant information is buried under fresher, more relevant, more positive content. Most people checking you out online won't go back more than a few pages of Google (or other search engine) references.
    Wait it out - Take no active measures to hide or delete the content, but let nature take its course. We recommend this approach only if you write, speak, or blog fairly often, creating a high volume of new online activity.

Also, watch out for connections between your more personal social networking activities and professional ones. Connecting your various online profiles must also be handled carefully to protect your brand. Many sites allow you to connect one profile to another. For example, you can have your LinkedIn profile connected to your Facebook profile and your Twitter account. But keep in mind your basic audience. If your Facebook postings are mostly personal, do not link your Facebook profile to your LinkedIn profile. However, if your Twitter postings are of a professional nature, then it makes sense to have them connected to your LinkedIn account.

Even if you do keep personal and business social networking separate, though, it’s best to be extra careful in your personal networking venues. Remove any unprofessional pictures of yourself, ask your friends to delete the tags to you in their pictures, ensure your privacy settings on social networking sites are set carefully, and edit any sites that allow you to eliminate negative content. Make any “about me” sections as professional as you can. Be ready to explain or defend negative information. Knowing ahead of time what a hiring manager might ask in an interview will help you be prepared to correct inaccurate data.

Blogs

A blog is a website that takes the form of an interactive conversation. The term "blog" is short for "web log,” another word for online journals. If you have your own blog, you write, or “post,” commentary on a specific subject, or group of related subjects, ideally connected to your career field, profession, or industry.  Readers of your blog can make comments on your posts, thus the conversation gets going.

Whether you participate as a blog owner and author or just comment on other people’s blogs, you can stay in touch with latest developments and thought leadership in the field you’ve targeted for your next career move and can establish your own visibility and credibility. Using blogging as a part of your job search strategy lets you demonstrate expertise in your career field and shows that you are an engaged participant in your professional community.

Should I Create a Blog or Only Comment on Other Blogs?

Blogs are free or inexpensive (depending on the service you use) and easy to set up but do take some effort to maintain to keep the conversation flowing. Commenting on other people’s blogs is also free and even easier to keep up with since you’re not the owner of the blog, just an occasional – or regular – reader and commenter.

The decision of whether or not to create a blog should be based on the quality and uniqueness of information that you want to share, the audience you’re targeting, and the amount of time you are able to commit to managing it.
Blog with Caution

When blogging, remember that anyone on the Internet can access the information you’ve posted on your blog. Pay careful attention to how you express yourself in a blog and manage the content you post diligently because it says a lot about you. Your level of expertise and writing skills are demonstrated, and your opinions and feelings will be evident as well. Readers will form an impression of you based on your posts and comments. Use your blog or your comments on others’ blogs to create value for the readers and avoid sharing controversial opinions or being negative. Consider that once your information is out there in the blogosphere, the link to your posts can be forwarded to others, mentioned in other people’s blogs, and even tweeted to people on Twitter. (See the subtopic Twitter.)
Promoting your Blog

To promote your blog, include the URL on your job search business cards, list it in your LinkedIn profile, (see the subtopic LinkedIn), or mention your blog to people at industry-related events and when you network offline. You can also use Twitter to attract attention to your blog.

To help others find your blog, you can have your blog indexed by Google.    



Personal Websites

In addition to participating in interactive online media, you may want to consider having static information about you online--static in that people don't interact with you but simply read what you've put online.  Personal websites are often referred to as webfolios and can include your bio, résumé, and achievements.  You can opt to hire a professional web developer or do it yourself.  These sites are easy to develop and are inexpensive.  By using a template provided by a hosting company, there are just a few things you need to address:

Your objective: What message do you want to convey?  Will this site be essentially an online résumé or do you want to establish a professional presence in a broader sense?

What defines you: What do you have to offer?  Gather any material that describes who you are.

What makes you marketable: Talk about your strengths, talents, and value-add qualities. Be concise and make an impact.

Reflect your brand: What colours, graphics, layout, and font represent you, your profession, and your message?  The look of the site is as important as your words.

Personal web pages may be as simple as a single page or as elaborate as an online database.  The content of personal web pages can vary and, depending on the hosting server, may contain images, text, and hyperlinks.  Below are tips for creating content that your readers will read.

    Keep sentences and paragraphs short.
    Start paragraphs with a key idea and then develop the point.
    Break up text with subheadings rather than long blocks of text.
    Use bullet-point lists.
    Use action language.
    Proofread your content.
    Have blank space around your text.

A few web-hosting sites you might want to consider using include: YAHOO! Web Hosting, Google Site, Go Daddy, and Network Solutions.




Facebook

Facebook is a social media site that was launched in 2004 and now has over 500 million active users internationally with about 50% of them logging in on any given day.  Approximately 70% of Facebook users are outside of the United States, and the site has been translated into more than 70 languages.  While primarily a social media site for keeping up with personal friends, the site has increasingly taken its place as a serious contender in the professional networking arena. Facebook can enhance networking, research, and finding jobs. 
Terminology

Understanding the language of each unique social media site is important as there is no universal language.  Facebook, like LinkedIn, Twitter, and other sites, has developed its own individual language of terms.  Important terms and their definition can be found in the Career Library document Online Networking Terms.
Furthering Your Online Brand with Facebook

The subject of creating and cleaning your online brand is perhaps never more applicable than on Facebook, which most consider primarily personal in nature.  Every post on the web, including Facebook, establishes your professional brand or, in some cases, distracts from it.  Just because you might feel like your activities on Facebook are worlds away from your professional networking, do not get overly comfortable; continue to be mindful of your professional reputation.  No matter how carefully you’ve controlled your privacy settings, every status update you post, photo you upload, link you share, or whatever language you use reflects your values, beliefs, and views and leaves a digital imprint; users, including potential hiring managers and recruiters, may gain access to your information in ways you didn’t anticipate and might view these differently from your intent. 

If you are choosing to use Facebook for career-related networking, take control of what you can impact. Completing your profile is one of the first things you do as a part of your branding.  List your work history over the last 10 years and add some key accomplishments, along with your education.  For security reasons, think carefully before posting personal contact information such as phone numbers or a full street address.  And when it comes to your birthday, you may choose to list your birth date; however we do not recommend posting your year of birth.
Searching for Jobs

Most Facebook users do not know how to use the site for job search.  The search function is the tool that helps you leverage the broader reach of the Facebook population beyond your own friends’ list. 

    Entering a term in the search function on your homepage allows you to explore the application directory using ‘job search’, ‘careers,’ or other related terms. 
    Using the search feature you can go to the ‘Marketplace’ and visit the jobs tab to look at thousands of jobs.  Search jobs by category, job title, industry, company, location, etc.  
    Jobs may also be found on the jobs tab of some company Facebook pages.

Research and Networking Functions

There are numerous ways one can research and network on Facebook.  Companies today are using Facebook fan pages to supplement their brand and reach potential consumers and employees.  Through a Fan Page you can become familiar with a company’s brand, see their values and culture reflected in the items posted, see job opportunities, and potentially access company employees. 

Networking using the search function allows you to tap additional resources and expand your online exposure.  Download the Advanced Search application and you will have deeper search access within the Facebook community.   Search the community by target companies, industry, and job title; discover who you can contact and/or friend.  Utilize these connections to explore what a company is like behind its brand, what the hiring process looks like, which recruiting firms the company uses, what they value in new hires, their internal language, and how your skills and background match up with their needs.

Don’t forget to check out Groups using the search tool, and join professional, alumni, industry, social, and jobs groups.  This delivers even more potential networking options.
Other Facebook Strategies

    Regularly post positive updates on your job search progress, ask for possible contacts, or share information.  This is a great way to help a recruiter find candidates for a job for which you have no interest but know someone who would.
    Use the Easy CV application to add a summary of your resume on your profile, and a dedicated page for the complete resume. 
    Follow professional events posted on Facebook.
    Join discussion on the walls of your target companies.
    Consider creating a personal targeted ad on Facebook.  This feature has the ability to target who will see your ad using filters such as location, keywords, and workplaces.

Whichever strategies you use, just be sure that you keep personal life and professional life separate, and use Facebook in a way that helps advance, not hinder, your career



LinkedIn

Over the past several years, LinkedIn has emerged as a powerhouse in the world of online professional networking.  Now a go-to sourcing tool for recruiters and corporations, it is also regarded a critical partner in career management by job seekers and employed professionals alike. 

The premise behind LinkedIn is simple – users from all over the world can join the site for free, create a profile that showcases their professional skills and history, and research or network with other users. 

For job seekers in particular, the ready access to professionals at a vast number of companies and visibility into the personal networks of other contacts makes LinkedIn a goldmine of potential opportunity.  You can begin to tap into this network as soon as you join the site and get started on your profile.

Terminology

Each social media site, including LinkedIn, has its own unique language.  Key terms and definitions can be found in the Career Library document Social Media Terms.

Building a Profile

Your LinkedIn profile will capture much of the same information as your resume and can serve as a significant element of your online personal brand.  Taking the time to create a robust and targeted profile will increase your chances of being found by recruiters and help your contacts understand your expertise.  Elements of a LinkedIn profile include past positions and accomplishments, specialties, and recommendations from professional contacts. 

Because recruiters often search for potential candidates by keywords in their LinkedIn profile, it is important that your profile be targeted and consistent.  Much like your resume, incorporate keywords and phrases that may be used by a recruiter to find someone with your skills.  In particular, focus on the Headline, Current and Past Position titles, and Summary/Specialties sections as these hold the most weight in keyword searches.

For more detailed information on building a strategic profile, use the Tips for a Powerful Profile guide in the Career Library.

Creating a Strategic Network

Building a strategic and robust network of connections is a crucial part of your LinkedIn usage – the size and strength of your network directly affects your access to people and information within LinkedIn.

Before you start sending or accepting connection requests, choose a strategy for your LinkedIn network.  Some people decide to create a large network and connect with users they don’t know; this strategy gives you greater access to information within LinkedIn, but a lack of trust with your connections could limit networking.  Others choose to maintain a close network of people they know well who can vouch for their professional accomplishments; this approach ensures a strong network but may limit your reach to others.  Consider how you want to use your network, both in job search and for ongoing career management, when choosing your approach.

As you build your network, add a variety of contacts, including those you know from past employers, associations, schools, volunteer organizations, and your personal network.  Your overall network will grow rapidly with each 1st degree connection you add.  This is due to the fact that you will also be gaining access to each of your 1st degree connections’ personal networks (now your 2nd degree connections) and the networks of each of those people (now your 3rd degree connections).  The power of LinkedIn often lies in your 2nd degree connections as this group is exponentially larger than your immediate network, and access to them is only an introduction away.  

Job Search

The vast number of people and company information available through LinkedIn can make the site an indispensible resource for job search.  Here are just some of the ways to use it strategically:

    Find and connect with people in a target company or industry. This can be a great way to learn about hidden opportunities, gain a referral or gather new insight.

    Prepare for an interview or networking meeting by researching your contacts’ LinkedIn profiles and discovering mutual connections or affiliations.

    Use your status update to keep your contacts current on your job search, share information, and ask for referrals.

    Use LinkedIn’s job board to locate exclusive job postings and those from partner sites; sometimes a contact name will be revealed in the job posting.

    Use the Company research tab to view company profile pages, employee information, and interesting company statistics compiled from user profiles.

    Engage in LinkedIn groups to share information and become more visible to recruiters. 

These ideas represent only a fraction of the ways that LinkedIn can be helpful in your job search.  For more targeted search results, be sure to utilize the ‘advanced’ option when searching for people; some of the best search fields to use can be Postal Code, Title, Company, Industry, and Relationship. 

Leveraging Groups

Joining and participating in some of LinkedIn’s almost one million groups is an important, but often overlooked, way to network, expand your reach, and get noticed on the site.

Want free messaging access to others on LinkedIn? Join a group that they are in!  One of LinkedIn’s greatest gems is the free messaging it makes available between group members. 

Want greater visibility to recruiters? Join the career groups for your target companies and be an active contributor.  And, get noticed by recruiters in non-company groups by participating in discussions or sending them a message.  Don’t forget to keep track of the group job board also. 

Want to follow the activity of group members without adding them to your network? Select the ‘follow’ option next to interesting fellow members to be notified of their activity within the group.  This can include jobs they post or discussions they start. 

Because LinkedIn limits you to membership in 50 groups at a time, join a variety of groups to create a combination of wide reach and niche visibility.  Some examples of groups to join are: specialty or certification groups, job seeker groups, local groups, company career groups, and alumni or professional groups.





Social Media for Job Search

Social media networking is much like traditional networking. It’s a way to build and maintain professional relationships so you can gather and share advice, information, and referrals. To understand this form of networking, let’s start with a definition of social media: Web-based tools that enable users to generate and share content and to engage in multi-directional conversations with others to interact, collaborate, and communicate.  There are hundreds of social media websites, but we focus on the three most popular, the ones we recommend you use for enhancing your online networking activities: LinkedIn, Facebook, and Twitter. So, what does this mean for you?

Whether you’re comfortable using technology or not, it is very important that you harness the power of social media to maximize your networking and job opportunities. Don’t worry if you’re not technologically savvy. We have several documents to walk you through getting started on LinkedIn, Facebook and Twitter.

In today’s marketplace, you need every advantage; utilizing these sites and building an online presence will enable you to increase your networking and build relationships that go well beyond what you are able to do with traditional  (face-to-face and telephone) networking. Social media networking is not meant to replace traditional networking but to give you a full arsenal of networking tools and ways to connect to groups and online communities.

Online networking is important in a job search but also plays a critical role in ongoing career management. After you land a job, it's an efficient way to continue professional networking when the time constraints of your work can make it difficult to keep up with traditional networking.

How important is social media networking? The figures continue to rise, but currently more than 80% of companies use LinkedIn for recruiting, 55% use Facebook, and 45% use Twitter. Don’t wait to get left behind!  (To learn more about these sites, refer to the Topic Resources gadget to the right and select Career Links.)
