==Process Improvements==
Process Improvements generally need to be an ongoing activity.
===Who Manages Process Improvement===
Process Improvement projects need to do the following:
*establish and maintain accountability
*create a system of process improvement management
*ensure company wide participation
*encourage innovative improvement solutions
*encourage delegation of duties and responsibilities
====Process Champion and Process Owner====
The ''Process Champion'' is the top dog.  Usually leads the process improvement team.
*process design
*ensures only 1 version is implemented
*delegates details of design changes to the improvement team
*must know organization, its culture, employees and customers
*ensures improvement is based on organization needs
*reviews and evaluates the improvement plan, the vision for improvement
*establishes customized activities
*responsible for making design decisions

The ''Process Owner'' is responsible for:
*making process activity decisions
*communicating decisions
*seeking resources
*ensuring effectiveness and efficiency

====Process Improvement Team====
The team will spend at least 20% of their time communicating with stakeholders and SMEs.

The team will include
*customer representatives
*management reps
*department reps
*leader
*sponsor

The process team must translate the design vision into a workable form.  They need to:
*communicate changes
*represent departments
*manage department changes
*conduct process observations
*update measurements
*circulate updated documentation
*establish and run sub process teams
*provide an improvement overview
====Sponsors and Project Leaders====
Sponsors provide the resources and support to the improvement effort.
*defines project objectives
*helps project leaders establish scope
*secure resources
*provide direction for specific projects
*communicates with others.

The project leader keeps the team on track.
*manage specific projects
*document project issues
*record process design decisions
*train employees
Project leaders are not project managers in the true sense of the term.  There may be more than one project leader

Project leaders must:
*focused on the day to day needs of the team
*needs a running list of live issues.  Includes info on the nature of the issue and the magnitude of the concern.
*keep track of process design decisions.  Can be recorded in a decision log.
*manages the project but not the department.

====Why Measure====

*quantify variations that are causing problems

====What to Measure====
#only measure what is important
#measure both internal and external performance
##How does the internal (business) think things are going?
##external measure would be things like customer satisfaction, or market share.
Emphasizing internal measures over external measures may annoy customers, where as emphasizing external over internal may make it more expensive to keep customers happy than their happiness is worth.

You can't measure everything.  Determining what to measure is often a compromise between value and feasibility.
====How to Measure====
*use a family of measures.
**productivity
***how many.  Measured in units
**quality
***How well.  Measured using capability indices.
**financial
***How much.  Dollars, cost, budgets, profits.
**timeliness
***How long/how fast.  Measured in duration, cycle time, shipping time, delivery time.

Focus on data that is available, accessible and obtainable.  Some data that may be valuable to your project may be too expensive or difficult to gather.

Using a family of measures allow you to holistically measure performance in multiple dimensions.  This helps prevent improving one area at the expense of another.

===Flowcharting===
Brainstorming the workflow shows where gaps and redundancies are.

Brainstorming also helps understand the true process.  You can't improve the process if you don't understand it.

Start with a high level view and then dig deeper one layer at a a time.

A diagram is easier to understand than just reading a text description.

Steps are
#brainstorm the process as it is
##tasks must be honestly described as they are, not as they should be
##ask what happens next
##ask who is involved
#record each process step
##you can use sticky notes or flowcharting software.  
###sticky notes is easy, and allows you to throw away duplicate steps.
###software creates the graphics as the group goes along.  Beneficial to see the relationship amongst steps.
#record entry requirements, inputs and deliverables of each step
##what data is needed to perform the step
##where does the data come from
##what tools are needed
##what version of software
##where does the output go
##what is the output
#arrange the steps in an agreed upon sequence
##sticky notes would be moved around until everyone agrees the steps are in order.
#verify the documented process by walking through the actual process.

====Activity flowchart====
An activity flowchart shows the activities.  Also called a process flowchart or a linear flowchart.

An Activity flowchart leaves room for Value Add and Non Value Add notes to be added.  A non value add step is one that a customer would not pay for.  These can often be eliminated.

A value add step is one that a customer would be glad to pay for.  Question to ask is does it improve or enhance the process for that step to be present.

Using flowcharts is especially helpful in identifying how much time each step takes, and how much idle time is between each step.  This can help you streamline the process.
====Deployment flowchart====
Shows which functional areas are responsible for the activities.  Also known as swimlanes.

This type of flowchart helps identify where problems exist when work is passed from group to group.

Swimlanes are often used at macro level but they become very busy with details at lower levels so aren't usually used.

===Inputs and outputs===
Customers remember the exceptions more than the norm.

Outputs are a function of upstream factors.  Outputs are a sum of inputs.  Fixing the inputs fixes the outputs.

Measuring allow you identify where the inputs are inconsistent or unpredictable.

For this stage you would use the cause and effect diagram (also known as fishbone or Ishikawa diagram).  This diagram helps identify cause and effect.
#condition (state of being)
#event
#action (process)

The condition, event or action that causes the issue is called the root cause.

#First step is to write the problem statement and the success criteria on the diagram (right hand side).
#Second step is to write down the conditions, events and actions that lead to the problem.
##brainstorm
##categorize - group together common ideas
###ensure that they are written as causes and not solutions
##remove redundancies.
##note as Constant, Noise or Experimental.
###constants must not change.  Must have SOPs or work instructions.
###Noise are inputs that you have no control over
###experimentals will undergo testing to find the optimal settings.

Drill down asking "why" or "so what".  Keep probing until you can't go any further.

The causes are analyzed and ranked to show how strongly they correlate to the problem.

A prioritization matrix are created from the cause and effect diagram.  It shows the correlation to the problem.

#choose a scale for correlation
#choose the weight of the correlation
#measure the frequency of defects
#choose the weight of the frequency
#multiply the correlation by its weight and the defects by its weight for each
#sum up the two multiples for each cause
#determine how you can eliminate Noise variables
##can any of these be changed to constants
##what is the time and cost
##can the SOP be written?
#determine if steps can be error-proofed.

===Value Add Assessments===
Value Add comes from the manufacturing sector where they take raw goods (e.g. steel) and change it adding value to it (making an auto).

Make sure each process adds to the product -> Value added = value after processing - value before processing.  If the value added is less than the cost for the process, this is wasted money.  Take into account
*necessary to production
**Needed to go forward, there may not be a direct add, but without them other added value is blocked
*customer requirements
**Meeting customer needs e.g. take order or ship product.
*business value added
**Don't meet customer needs but are required by legal needs e.g. employee record maintenance.
**minimize the cost.
*no value added
**reviews, approvals, storage, movement.
**eliminate if possible
