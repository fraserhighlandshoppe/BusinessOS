YOUR MARKETING: Session 2
Edgy Authenticity, Variation, And
The Loyalty Ladder
In this session, you'll learn how being "edgy authentic" can take your marketing to the next level,
how to avoid over-reacting to the natural ups and downs in your business, and how to leverage the
diﬀerent loyalty levels in your customers.

• One important concept is the idea of "edgy authenticity."
• Let's say my niche is helping people get out of debt. Maybe in the culture I live in, "avoiding
bankruptcy" is the way everyone refers to this topic, and no one really wants to push further than
that. But I want to be authentic and edgy... I want to be a little over the edge. I would ask myself,
"What can I say this is true for me, but would zing people a little bit, push the boundaries of their
comfort zone, and raise a few eyebrows... without going TOO far?"

• So I ask myself , "What's the biggest fear of someone going bankrupt?" I come to the idea that
having your spouse leave you is the hot button here. Culturally it's not cool to say "your spouse is
going to leave you for someone with money." But I've decided that's the hot button, so I say, "The
night before I had to file bankruptcy, all I could think about was my spouse sleeping with some
geeky rich guy. It sucked."

• It's usually not okay to say this, but the fact I'm talking about my own experience makes it more
acceptable. It's definitely in that "edgy" zone. It will grab attention.

• Another key principle comes from Edward Deming. He wasn't a marketing guy, but his concepts
of variation and systems thinking are very valuable to marketing.

• The basic idea of variation is if you run a physical process in the real world, it's not going to be
100% predictable. The physical universe adds a little bit of the random factor and messes with the
process.

• The lesson here is there is always variation. In terms of marketing, if 1000 people see an ad and 7
people respond, you could conclude that you get 7% response to this ad. But Deming would say
you need a bigger sample size before you get a reliable result.

• Another application of this is to realize that certain days are better than others. When most
business people have a great day, they party and try to figure out what they did right. When they
have a bad day, they criticize people and try to figure out what they did wrong. But really all that's
happening is natural variation.

• A solution is to look at the 30 day moving average instead of daily results. That'll keep you from
driving your business with a joy stick.

• Robert Collier says enter into the conversation the prospect is already having in their mind.
Almost none of the marketing you see will you say, "Now that seems like the conversation going
on inside the prospect's mind." Most marketers don't get this right.

• Murray Raphel wrote a book called Up The Loyalty Ladder. What Murray introduced me to is the
idea that loyalty is a continuum. Customers are not either loyal or not loyal - they are diﬀerent
levels of loyal. Here's the ladder: A suspect is a person who you don't know if they're a good
match for your product. A prospect has raised their hand and indicated they might be interested
in buying what you're selling. A customer has purchased once. A client has purchased multiple
times. An advocate tells other people to buy your stuﬀ.

• Here's one way to look at the power of paying attention to the Loyalty Ladder:
Let's say a real estate agent was selling fifty $200,000 homes a year. That's $10,000,000 a year in
gross revenue. If you ask them how much they spend on marketing, they might spend $5000 a
month on marketing, with $4000 on print and display ads, $500 on flyers, and $500 on a direct
mail piece to their past customers.

• In the past year, of their 50 customers, let's say 30 of them came from referrals from other people,
10 came from open houses, and 10 from the ads. So, 60% of their business came from referrals.
40% came from advertising. But that's not how they're spending their money. 60% of their
customers are coming from 10% of their marketing budget. What would happen if they doubled
the amount they spent on customers, devoting 20% of their advertising budget on them? Would
they double the number of homes they sold from referrals next year, selling 60 instead of 30?

• Here's the next level: If you ask them, of the referral sales, how many people referred them to you?
Maybe one person referred 7, another referred 4, etc.. What if you went "hog wild" here and spend
$1000 a month on the 10 people who refer you the most sales, sending each a $100 gift every
month and thanking them for referring people. What would the result be? Could be a tremendous
jump in sales.
