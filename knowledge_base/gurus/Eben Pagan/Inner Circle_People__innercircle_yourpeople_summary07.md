YOUR PEOPLE: Session 7
An Interviewing Process That Fills Your
Team With Stars
In this session, you’ll learn the interviewing process we use to screen out C-Players and attract APlayers, a specific series of questions that’ll give you psychological insight into who you’re
interviewing (and get them to reveal things about themselves they’d never admit), and the danger of
relying on online personality assessments in your interviewing process.

• If you want to do something significant with your business, it’s going to involve other people…
working for you, partnering, selling stuﬀ for you, etc. The more you understand people and how
they’re wired, the better judge of people you’ll be, the better you’ll know what roles to put people
in, and the better able you’ll be able to avoid hiring disasters.

• You don’t have to be like me and Wyatt and spend way too much time studying this stuﬀ and take
time away from your business. But we’re trying to show you where you can invest your “learning
energy” so you can wake up with a whole new set of people tools 3 or 6 months down the road.
It’ll be like you can see the Matrix and everyone else can only see in 2 dimensions.

• So you’ve decided who you want, you’re building a virtual bench, and now you’ve narrowed it
down to someone you want to interview. How do you do an interview? The best interviewing
process I know of comes from Brad Smart and “Topgrading”. I interviewed Brad – do your best to
hear that interview. It could be the most valuable hour of anything I’ve heard in business. He
summaries years of research and wisdom.

• The core of the Topgrading Interview process is a serial, in-depth career history. This is one of the
most ingenious things I’ve heard. The idea is that you’re going to ask the other person about
every work experience they’ve had, in order, and ask them a series of questions about each one.
The idea behind this is if you ask the same questions about one work experience, and the next,
and the next, patterns will emerge… patterns that they don’t even realize they’re sharing with
you. They think it’s about the content of their answers, but they don’t know that you’re looking for
the patterns between work experiences. As these patterns emerge, there are some things for you
to keep in mind.

• For example, A-players tend to have work experiences that show them to be consistent, hard
working, and resourceful. C-players blame the world for their failures, lack initiative, whine and
complain, and are good at creating job security but not at creating results.

• Brad recommends you record the answers and then write a report. If you want to get really fancy,
do a tandem Topgrading interview. This is an in-depth 2-4 hour interview. One person interviews,
one person takes notes. This allows you to have one person ask all the questions so they can really
drill down into particular areas, because they have a note taker that’s capturing everything. Then
after, the two get together and write a report. This process is VERY powerful. It’s also a great team
building exercise.

• You want to figure out why a person got a job in the first place, what their expectations were,
what their successes were, what their failures were, what their boss would say their strengths and
weaknesses were, and why they left. If they had certain expectations that weren’t met, perhaps
they tend to have unrealistic expectations.

•

C-players will blame other people for their failures. A-players will say success was a group eﬀort,
failures were their responsibility. Also, A-players usually leave because they needed to grow in
their career and they had bigger and better opportunities elsewhere.

• Some business owners rely on online personality assessments to get a feel for their potential
hires. My experience with online personality tests is that they don’t always provide the value you
thought they would. The reason is because most people answer tests like these with who they
think you want them to be, not who they really are. And since most people haven’t spent the time
to really understand the diﬀerence between what a person says and what a person does, business
owners and managers have a hard time accurately interpreting the test results. Plus, once you
“know” someone’s personality type, it’s easy to start putting them in boxes and interact with their
type instead of who they really are.
