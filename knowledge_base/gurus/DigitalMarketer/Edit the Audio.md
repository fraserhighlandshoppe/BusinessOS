Edit the Audio
In the audio stage, you want to make the podcast episode as engaging as possible. You’re looking for the “magic” so to speak.
Here, the audio editor will attempt to CAPTURE or CREATE energy, chemistry, and passion, while highlighting the host’s (or hosts’, in our case) personality. This is what people like to listen to.
The editor will go through the episode and remove anything that’s going to distract from the story or the message of the episode.
When editing, remember that content is king. Build the episode around the content and the story.
For us, once the recording is finished, we upload the recordings to a shared file in Dropbox. We send three audio files, one from each host, to the editor. That way, if there’s an issue with one of the files or a background noise in one file, the editor can pick and choose the best version.
Our audio editor, Darren, downloads the recording and puts it into Logic Pro. There’s other digital audio editors you can use, such as: Pro Tools, Audacity (which is free), Adobe Audition, etc. They’re all fairly similar, but, in our opinion, Logic Pro is a great one, which costs about $200.
After editing, the episode is made into a MP3 file, and sent along the production line to be made live. We’ll get into the editing steps in just a moment, but first, let’s talk about equipment…
The mics you use are very important. They capture your voice and determine if it will be pleasing or horrible to listen to.
There’s two main kinds of mics you can use: the condenser microphone or the dynamic microphone. Currently, we use dynamic mics for podcasting, because they reject a lot of ambient noise. Condenser microphones are more designed for recording in a studio.
The mics we use are called Rode Podcasters, which you can acquire from many different retailers for approximately $229.
A less expensive mic, but still of good quality, is the ATR-2100, which you can purchase from a multitude of retailers for a little over $59. We started off with the ATR mics and then upgraded to the Rode Podcasters.
Next, we work with a music producer to help create an audio brand for Perpetual Traffic and set the tone. Just like graphic designers build a visual brand with colors, fonts, and images, a music producer builds a brand with sounds and instruments that are signature and custom to your product.
Our music producer, who also happens to be our audio editor, has helped us develop the mood of our podcast, and designed signature music themes for us to draw from and use in the podcast. This helps to create excitement or spark curiosity in an episode.
However, you don’t need a music producer for your podcast. If you’re on a different budget than us, or if you’re a hobbyist musician, you can license music from a third party such as AudioJungle for your podcast music needs. You can also use the built in Apple loops from within Logic Pro.
Now, let’s talk about the six steps our audio editor follows to edit an episode…
How We Take It from Raw Files to Finished Episode
Step 1: Major Edits
Listen through and chop out the main issues, such as dead air. Here, you’re also looking to get rid of the fluff and filler, and to get to the main points as quickly as possible.
Your listeners are busy, and there’s lots pulling on their attention. To grab and keep their attention, you need to make the episode as concise and engaging as possible.
In this step, the editor may also rearrange the episode – moving sections around to improve the overall flow.
Step 2: Fine Details Edit
This is where you’ll tighten up sentences, removing “ums” or pauses for instance. The editor will also remove laughter that goes on too long or cut swear words.
The goal is to make the speakers sound as polished as possible.
Step 3: Audio File Cleanup
Next, the editor will repair, enhance, and restore problematic audio files – from microphone bumps to inconsistent levels.
To do this, we use Izotope RX 5 to really clean up the audio file. For instance, if there’s a dog barking or a car horn in the background, you can isolate it and remove it, just as if you were snipping something out in Photoshop.
The Advanced RX 5 version we use runs for around $1,200, but another version can be obtained for about $350.
Step 4: Use Music to Shape the Episode
Add music to your podcast episodes to…
Add energy and create mood.
Provide transitions and segues between topics.
Highlight important points made.
Provide a “brain break” after a section of very technical information.
Cover bad audio that otherwise can’t be fixed.
Make sure any music you put in the episode serves the content and the story. If the music is too jarring it can turn listeners off and take them out of the show.
For instance, in the first episodes of Perpetual Traffic, some people commented in the reviews that they didn’t like the “disco” music. So we went back, analyzed how we used music throughout episodes, and adjusted accordingly.
Step 5: Polishing the Audio
This is the finishing touches to make everyone on the episode sound good. Here, the editor will…
Equalize the voices – Pull out the problem areas in the low, low sounds and frequencies and the high, high sounds and frequencies. This will help to clean up the sound.
Compress the voices – This gives you the “radio ready” sound and every voice benefits from it.
Equalize the voices, again – Boosting the base and treble (the lows and the highs) to give the voices a pleasing, warm sound. Here’s an example of what equalizing one of our host’s, Keith, voice looks like.
Use a limiter on all audio tracks – This makes the all the audio, from the voices to the music, of the episode all at the same level, so the listener doesn’t have to continuously turn their volume up and down throughout the show.
Step 6: Find an Intro Quote
Lastly, the editor will do a final run through to make sure there aren’t any mistakes. Our editor also uses this as an opportunity to find a quote to open the show with. The intro quote serves as a teaser or a synopsis of the episode content, and helps to pull listeners in.
The audio editor sends the finished podcast episode to us via WeTransfer, an easy platform to send audio files that has both free and a paid service options.
While you don’t need everything we discussed in this module to make your podcast work, we wanted to show you the technical side. Investing in an audio editor will both save you time, and make your podcast sound professional.
