Why Real Estate Investors Fail 4 Key Observations: Part 1 of 2
Looking at both sides of the coin is critical to creating
successful real estate joint ventures. This month we
are going to look at the ‘other side of the real estate
investing coin’.
This article comes from contributions from William
Charlton who has been a mortgage lender in Alberta
for the past decade. He has a
passion for mortgages and real estate, William focuses his mortgage business toward real estate investors. You can reach him
at william@wrref.ca or 403.701.7766.
In this article we will share the first two observations
and next month we will conclude with the final observations.
Many books and articles have been written on “‘how to
be a successful real estate investor”&#39;. However,
after spending the past decade working with hundreds
of real estate investors, I’ve seen many failed investments. So I gave it some thought as to why real estate
investors fail. Here are my observations:
During the 2005-2006 Alberta real estate boom, home
prices were rising double digits monthly. ANYONE
could make money buying real estate and selling for a
profit just by simply buying a house for asking price
and by the time they took possession it had already
appreciated. These people fall into under reason Observation #1.
1) They Got Lucky in a Good Market.
These people became speculators and not real estate
investors. They bought in after watching a half hour TV
show. They didn’t take the time to obtain valuable real
estate education. They didn’t have an understanding of
the changing market conditions, what makes a good
deal vs. what makes a risky deal. They didn’t know
which properties to avoid or how to refinance and rent
the property to create cash flow in a down market.
After making a lot of money in the great market of 2005
-2006 they continued to buy property in 2007 until,
suddenly, the price of their fully priced home started to
fall before they could sell it. They were speculators
with no backup plan. They were buying property without rental cash flow potential and buying property in
bad locations. They had early, easy success but started to overextend themselves. Many experienced significant losses.
A few lucky speculators who happened to own good
lower priced property were able to rent out the property
to make the mortgage

payments, but still faced
hard financial times.
As a mortgage lender,
the investors I watched lose
the most money had owned
property they couldn’t rent to cover mortgage payments (homes valued over $600,000) and/or owned
property away from transit and jobs (rural, small
towns, acreages, high-end homes outside of a city’s
core etc.). Write this down and revert back when you
start to see home prices start to depreciate – you’ll
quickly see what I am talking about!
We were in another really good market in Alberta in
2014 and saw it again. Investors buying marginal
deals just barely squeaking out a profit or not cash
flowing their rentals. The market is masking their
poor purchases. This leads to reason Observation
#2:
2) Being Too Emotional.
After thinking about the strong real estate investors
I’ve worked with, I tried to figure out some of their
common traits. The #1 trait I always come back to is
emotion. The very best remove emotion from their
real estate transactions. To them it is strictly business, strictly numbers, and all a matter of fact. They
don’t do marginal deals. They don’t settle for weak
numbers.
Top real estate investors do extensive due-diligence
on the economic and market conditions. They inform
themselves on what makes a good deal and a bad
deal in a specific niche buying area. They immerse
themselves in the values and specs of homes in a
particular area.
Then, they decide on a number that works for them to
purchase a home

in case one of your team members is unavailable?
Do you have a second lawyer who knows what
you do? What if the seller of a property you
want to buy uses the same lawyer as you? You
want them to feel comfortable with the transaction. Go to your second lawyer and do the deal.

recently has not been a problem in my business because I believe in having backup.

Long Distance Management
In an ideal world you would like to be able to purchase your investment property within a one hour
drive from your home so that you can have the option to manage the property yourself, should you
want to. When you are able to find and purchase an
investment property close to home then I always encourage my students to manage the property themselves for at least one year in order to learn what is
involved in property management.
You should always include a management fee in
your calculations based upon a realistic market rate,
when you purchase an investment property. Do this
even if you plan to self-manage simply because you
never know what the future holds for you and your
ability to manage the property long term. You need
to be sure there is enough cash flow to pay for management, if you need it. TIP – often times when owners list their property for sale they fail to include the
property management expense which therefore increases the cash flow and ultimately the asking
price.
The other reason to self-manage for at least one
year is so that you know and understand what a
good property manager should be doing for you. And
you will better appreciate what they actually do and
realize their worth. A good management company
makes property management look easy and when
this happens many land lords feel that they are over
paying for the service simply because they don’t see
or understand just how difficult the job really is.
Each Province/State will have different rules and
regulations with respect to regulating the Property
Management Industry so you need to be aware of
what these rules are. For example, in Ontario you do
not need to be licensed to manage other people’s
properties while in Alberta you do need to be licensed. This means that in Ontario you could be
handing over the keys to your investment to an inexperienced, non-licensed person who will be responsible for collecting thousands of dollars every month
on your behalf and representing you as a land lord.
This could be very risky and this is why you absolutely want to do your diligence and research the
company before handing over the keys.
On the flip side, just because a person is licensed
does not insure that the person is a good property
manager but at least they are supervised by a regulatory body and they must work under the umbrella
of the Real Estate Act. This means there is strict
monitoring of the company bank accounts where
your money is sitting and there are strict rules which

they must adhere to with
respect to how they manage
properties.
So why am I bringing this
topic up at this time? Well, a
friend of mine reached out to me for advice regarding his rental property. He is not a full time
investor and is not aware of all the rules and regulations regarding the management of his property
by someone else. He hired the services of another
active investor who is not licensed as a property
manager, thinking that this person should know
what they are doing. In all fairness, my friend was
unaware that in his province a Property Manager
needs to be licensed. My friend trusted the other
investor to manage the property well but instead
his friend allowed the tenant to do work on the
rental unit without the owner’s permission and
without proper permits. The Manager also altered
the lease agreement without my friend’s
knowledge and or permission. This has now put
my friend in a very vulnerable position because his
insurance could be void in the event of a fire or if
someone gets hurt on the property due to the illegal work performed by the Tenant. This could put
him at risk of being sued.
I have another friend who, years ago, employed
the service of what he believed to be a professional management company to manage his small
portfolio of properties. All went well for several
months until he started to receive notices for nonpayment of bills related to the properties, including
the mortgages. Then suddenly one day after all
rents were collected, the property manager
skipped town leaving my friend with all these unpaid bills and no rental income to pay them.

names of a few people you know who earn a high

yourself, “On a scale of 1 – 10 with 10 being excellent, how would I rate this person’s ability at sales?”

does this person actively engage in selling?” In other words, do they constantly and assertively promote things or seek to influence others? Write

see how your own personal score, regardless of
whether it is high or low, is directly correlated to
your income. My point is this. It doesn’t matter how
amazing your product or service is or how strongly
you desire to be more successful. Without sales
skills and activity you will simply never earn a lot of
money. This is not a good thing or a bad thing; it is
simply a fact of life!

Real Estate Investing University
There is no formal university education for real estate
investing. Or when you think about it, there are no
formal universities for any type of investing, in particular, investing profitably. There are ad hoc organizations that tempt you with instant get-rich quick
schemes but only the 1% even have the wherewithal
to repeat such a claim.
How much time and expense is involved in achieving
a Master’s Degree, a Bachelor’s Degree, a Technical
Diploma, a Certificate? The minimum requirement is
two years of full-time study to be familiar with the
course curriculum. This does not mean you have
mastered the curriculum.
The one crucial skill that an investor needs to develop is how to think counter to the majority. School
doesn’t teach you this. It starts by keeping an open
and curious mind, and questioning everything.
So you may understand why I am repeatedly amazed
at the casual manner in which some real estate investors approach investing. It takes time and effort to
study something of this importance in order to become competent; remember the 10,000 hours of
study and practice to become a master.
It is up to the individual investor to create their own
real estate syllabus because the type of investing
that you do depends on your character and values.
Once you have selected your electives, then put
yourself through a similar program of pedagogical
study that a post-secondary institution would create.
Learning about real estate investing needs to be systematic, not haphazard.
Real estate is one of the most expensive and appreciable assets that a person can own. There is a lot of
it and it’s not going anywhere. There is no need to
rush in and buy something right away because there
are months of research and due diligence that must
go toward your real estate education before you are
qualified to even make an offer.
And when you do analyze a deal, how do you really
know if it’s a lemon or a peach without having put in a
minimum of 160 hours of due diligence? Just because something appears to be cheap, doesn’t mean
it’s a great deal. There might a very good reason why
it’s priced under market.
This reminds me of a rookie investor who was so excited to land her first deal that she almost forgot to
have me vet the deal. It’s a good thing she did. The
price of the house was so low that it was an automatic red flag. There was a note in the inspection report
about a sink hole in the back yard. Another red flag.

After asking a few more
questions about the
neighbourhood, her research revealed that the
house was built over an
abandoned mine shaft.
After a few more search engines later, it became very
obvious why this owner would never be able to sell their
house except to a naïve buyer.
The cost to clean up environmental contamination is
borne by the current owner. If the current owner is unable to pay for the remediation, the government has the
authority to pursue all past owners for fixing the contamination even though it was the mining company who created the contamination in the first place. This is a very
unfair law but that’s the government.
Real estate is exciting and this is where trouble starts
because real estate investing is not a sporting event.
Cool heads must prevail. Expectations must remain flexible. Similar to young love, unrequited love sets you up
for failure. You cannot fall in love with the deal. So how
do you manage emotions so you can stay the course?
Surround yourself with mentors and advisors who are
more objective than you. Listen to their counsel. Keep a
curious mind.
This reminds me of another young gentleman just starting out in real estate investing. He went into foreclosure
on his first deal. He came to me asking for advice. It was
obvious that the numbers didn’t work. He had fallen in
love with the deal. To make matters even more embarrassing for him, he had already asked twelve mentors
before me for their advice. He had ignored all of them.
He was looking for a mentor to approve what he had
done. He had squandered a year in the foreclosure process with just two days left before he would be forced to
declare bankruptcy.

Lowest Rates I Have Seen!
– What Should I Choose?
Wow, Just when I thought interest rates could not go any lower. Last fall saw a bit of a spike
or jump in interest rates. Since
then they have slowly come down from there. We
are seeing fixed interest rates these days running
around the 2.45% mark for 5 years. I don’t ever
recall seeing the fixed rates this low. Two years
ago when my own personal mortgage came up for
a renewal I thought that 2.85% was amazing. So I
locked that in for 5 years thinking I I had hit the
perfect market timing. Only to see it quite a bit
lower here today. I am not really complaining
though. 2.85% is still a wonderful rate to have.
But what does come to mind is the product selection that I recommend these days. Five year variable rates are running around the 2.30% and with
only a 0.15% savings it really doesn’t make this
product a huge savings over the fixed rate product. I really think that if you are choosing a product
today it comes down to your personal risk tolerance and what you are planning to do with the
property.
For example: If you sleep much better at night
knowing that your interest rate on that mortgage
you have will not change over the next 5 years
then that is the right choice for you. The savings
you would gain by choosing a variable really
aren’t worth the stress.
However if you are looking for the best rate with
the best flexibility then you for sure will want to
choose the variable rate. Recall the idea behind
the variable rate savings is to squeeze out as
much savings during the first half of the 5 year
term so that if the rates climb higher in the second
half of your mortgage you break even. For the
past ten years at least this strategy has been the
way to go. Remember that with the variable rate
product a person can call their lender any time
during the mortgage term and ask them to change
it over to the fixed product and they will gladly do
that for you.
So if you are two years into your five year variable
rate term you can call in and fix the interest rate to
the best rate they offer at that time into the future.
The minimum term that you would be allowed to

Page 8

take would be the three year fixed rate at that
point. Why… Because we agreed to do business with this lender for the full five years. At
this time of switching we would only be two
years into our five year deal.
Another reason a person may choose to take
the variable rate product is simply the cost to
break the contract if any unsuspecting situation
happens and you are forced to sell the property
early. With a fixed rate mortgage these days it
can still cost a huge fee to break your mortgage
contract if you are in a locked in product. The
big five banks especially have a “posted rate
vs. discount” calculation that ends up costing
quite a bit of money if you are not careful. While
on the other hand if you are in a variable rate
mortgage and you have to sell your house for
any reason the most you would pay is three
months interest. Three months interest these
days usually ends up being three times half of
your regular monthly payment. Its quite nice
these days with around half of your new mortgage payment going to your principal reduction
right at the start of your twenty five or thirty year
mortgage. We can thank these wonderfully low
rates for that.
Any how it truly is a great time for low rates and
simply put the product you should choose really
has to do with your personal market risk tolerance and your future plans with that home.
I am always here to discuss any specific situation you might have of course!

LET’S GET CREATIVE
If none of these more traditional approaches suit your
particular circumstances, let’s talk about nontraditional concepts that may have a chance to help
you keep a property. Here are some creative options
that your non-creative, professional advisors may not
suggest:
Deal Sharing
If your issue on your personal residence is that you
can’t make payments, how about a joint venture? With
any decent amount of equity in your property, you can
sell a percentage of ownership to a joint venture partner. Once you have funds from selling an interest, you
can make up delinquent mortgage payments and perhaps give yourself a chance to get on your feet.
If foreclosure has already started, you will have to deal
with the lender as part of paying those delinquent payments along with the lender’s legal fees and any other
costs which when done will result in the foreclosure
stopping . Yes, now you have a partner in your home
but, that may be better than losing your home.
What about if you are an investor? If you just can’t rent
the property and because of personal circumstances
can’t make the mortgage payments, you might be able
to find a joint venture partner who would put up some
money to buy a portion of your equity which you can
then use to make the mortgage payments. However,
without any equity and for whatever reason, no tenants, you would have to make a very strong case for
future prospects to have any joint venturer put up
money to carry you through the tough times.
What About Selling Your Property?
This is the first and most obvious move in your chess
game with the lender. Sell, pay real estate commission, pay the penalties on your mortgage, pay your
legal fees and just be done with the property. This only
works if you actually have some equity in the property
that can pay your costs of sale.
Or, you can try and sell the property yourself. Or, you
can try to work with a reduced-commission realtor if
you have confidence they can sell, since commission
takes such a big chunk of your sale proceeds when
you have a small amount of equity.
In a rapidly deteriorating market, one key success factor is listing at a price that will actually attract buyers
and promote the sale. The object isn’t to squeeze every last penny from the sale. The object is to actually
sell! Many sellers make the mistake of listing their
property at last week’s or last month’s price. As prices

continue to drop, the sellers
have to continually adjust their
price downward, essentially
chasing today’s value but never
quite getting there. Eventually
sellers capitulate and sell at whatever number they can
sell at potentially reducing their sale proceeds substantially. Price your property to sell and take your beating.
An actual sale is what this option is all about.
Along with an outright sale you could in the proper circumstances, depending on a number of factors including
but not limited to:
 today’s property value in relation to your existing
mortgage,
 rentability,
 cash flow generation potential,
 suitability as personal residence,
look at selling to either another investor or to someone
looking to move in.
Sales techniques could be the Lease Option more often
described as a Rent To Own (RTO). Or, you could sell
by way of Agreement for Sale. None of these techniques will likely be effective if your existing mortgage
exceeds the value of the property by a substantial
amount. But, I have seen deals put together where
sellers with a mortgage principal $20,000 more than the
current value of the property actually contributed
$20,000 to the sale. They did not want their credit ruined
by going to foreclosure.
The ‘Negative Cash Flow’ Option
If your property is okay except for negative cash flow,
then in these tougher economic times, you might consider carrying $200, $300 or even $400 per month negative
cash flow for a substantial period of time.
You can especially consider this option if the alternative
is complete loss of the property and your substantial
original investment along with ruination of your credit. If
you bought a property for $300,000 with 20% down, that
is $60,000. If you were negative cash flow $300 per
month for two years that is $7,200.

Making The Most Of The
Basement Layout
When I was touring Western Canada last year with
my basement suite course
one of the most common
questions I would get is,
“How the heck are you
able to put a basement suite into a home for less than
$8000.00?” People couldn’t believe it! I remember one
lady even getting angry with me saying that I must be
lying. Well, I’m not, and as the students that went on
my property tour in Edmonton saw, I have done it time
and time again. So what’s the trick you ask? It’s quite
simple, if the layout doesn’t work for a basement suite
walk-away.

• A minimum 3pcs bathroom
Kitchens and bathrooms are usually your most expensive renovations. If you’re putting a suite in you can’t
avoid the kitchen cost so do your best to find an existing
adequate bathroom. Even converting a two-piece bathroom into a three can be quite onerous. It involves jackhammering the concrete and bringing in a drain, never a
cheap option.

People often try to take a home that has no business
having a basement suite and spend a small fortune
retrofitting it until it finally works as a double occupancy home. In my opinion, that’s wasted money, and it’s
going to take a lot of rent checks before you pay
back those costs. My approach is to follow the path of
least resistance. I’m looking for a home that is perfectly set-up for a basement suite; it just hasn’t been done
yet. The existing layout of the basement is crucial to
accomplishing this feat but when you find it you’ll save
yourself not only a pile of money but also a lot of time
& energy. Here are my top 6 must-haves when scoping out basements:

• Existing 100 AMP electrical service
You’ll need at least a 100 AMP service to run a basement suited home. Trying to upgrade your service after
you purchase the investment will cost you at-least a few
thousand bucks.

• An entirely separate entrance to the suite:
Usually an existing side or back door leading to the
basement is perfect. An example of a layout that is
difficult to make-work would be a 4 level splits. They
are nice because they usually have big windows in the
basement but unfortunately they typically have stairs
going directly to the basement from the front door. You
can retrofit this to work, but it’s going to cost a lot of
$$$.
• A common area for shared laundry access
Having to put in separate laundry on both floors can
sometimes be impossible as there is no good spot for
it in most main floor homes (at least not in the typical
older bungalows I’m buying). Even if there is a suitable
location, remember, that’s now two sets of laundry
you’ll have to maintain, not to mention the cost of
plumbing the lines and venting the dryer.


Two existing bedrooms
Or an appropriate spot to frame in a second bedroom. I can fit framing a second room within an
$8000 overall budget, but two rooms can get pretty challenging. As a rule of thumb, if there are no
bedrooms in the basement I typically walk away.

Page 10

• Room for a kitchen and living room
This one is usually easy enough to come across when
looking at basements. The only thing I would add is that
if you have your future kitchen location close to your
laundry area, you will save on some plumbing costs.

Stick to these layout restrictions and you’ll be taking
profits to the bank instead of using your rent checks to
pay down renovation debt much faster than the vast majority of real estate investors. Now before you go thinking this must be impossible to find these, I can tell you
from my personal experience they are out there waiting
for you to find them! Will you come across 5 per day?
Probably not. However, if you are consistently looking,
it’s realistic to find 10-15 per year in any major Canadian
market.
The number one reason people tend to stop buying revenue properties isn’t that they can’t figure out what to do
with the extra income it’s because they run out of money
to buy another. Keep your costs down, especially when
starting out. It will pay-off in spades when it comes to
funding your next deal. Remember, cash is king in the
real estate game so do your best to protect it!

Title-in-Lieu of Foreclosure (often called Deed-in-lieu in other jurisdictions)
Some owners have used this process to stop a foreclosure quickly after it has started or to
stop it from getting started. This process has you transferring the property back to the lender. You give the lender a transfer of land and sometimes a quit claim and the lender stops
the foreclosure.
Titles-in-lieu of foreclosure will probably ruin your credit to the same extent as a foreclosure
would, so there isn’t any real benefit there. Why would you do it? This method prevents or
ends the foreclosure process quickly and may give you some room to negotiate with your
lender. There are a number of situations where you should be able to negotiate.
In Alberta (but not in the rest of Canada) some mortgages are nonrecourse. Briefly, if you
have a conventional mortgage (that is, a mortgage not insured by CMHC or other insurer) in
your personal name, whether it is your personal residence or an investment property, all the
lender can do is foreclose and take away the property. If the property is worth less than the
mortgage, they can’t come after the homeowner for the deficiency. You might be able to turn
your nonrecourse mortgage into an advantage.
For example, I believe you have some room to negotiate if your property is substantially underwater and you have a non-recourse mortgage. Let’s say that the mortgage principal is
$40,000 more than the current value of the property. The lenders legal bill for a very plain
vanilla, no wrinkles foreclosure will be $2500-$5000. A smart lender will understand that if
they foreclose they will lose $42,500-$45,000 being the sum of the deficiency and their legal
costs. A smart lender would listen to your offer to pay the lender a portion of those uncollectible losses.
Remember, this is a negotiation. How much should you offer? Hard to say, this is a negotiation. For example, it might be a good deal for you if you paid the lender $10,000 and agreed
to transfer the title to the lender without a foreclosure with the further agreement by the lender not to make a credit report.
It might even be a good deal for the lender if they just got the property back without having
to spend $2500 - $5000 on their lawyer.
CAUTION, lenders often seem incapable of seeing what might be in their best interest.
All they understand is their basic policy when the mortgage is in default and they are incapable of stepping outside the policy. For any of these negotiated settlements to work, you will
have to move up the authority chain beyond your local branch or the lenders 800 number
‘help desk’.

Final Thoughts This Month:

Why Retirement Isn’t In The Cards For Me.
The whole idea of ‘retirement’ has been on my mind
lately. A very close friend is doing her nursing practicum, and working with a number of nurses. One of
them has it figured out to the day how long it will be
before she retires…to the DAY! She makes it sound
like a prison sentence. An attendee at one of my
Magnetic Investor Attraction Workshops asked me
“So Dave, are you retired yet?”. Seemed like a curious question seeing that it was during a break at a
workshop that I was leading… hence the answer
would seem obvious ;-). I responded that no, I am
not retired, and thanks to my divorce and a few other
financial mishaps, I am not currently in any position
to do so either.
But even if I were financially able to ‘retire’ right now,
I wouldn’t.
I think many working Canadians have a pretty idealistic view of what retirement is. They think it means
that they can sleep in every day, play golf or watch tv
as much as they want, and not have to answer to
anyone. And if they are properly set up for it, that
may be true.
But I have another term for it… Ultimate BOREDOM!
(And I might add in “a slow, painful, meaningless slippage into eternal darkness”).
I have actually tried a version of ‘retirement’ for an
extended period of time. When I was in my early

20’s I travelled around Mexico and Central America for
2 ½ years. That gave me a LOT of ‘slow down and
smell the roses’ time. Don’t get me wrong…it was fun
(a LOT of fun quite often), but it got to the point where it
was mind-numbingly BORING. It really got to the point
where I felt like I was frittering my life away and not
making any progress.
When I got to Costa Rica in 1992, I was ready to settle
down and get into doing something productive. That’s
when I started teaching English, and eventually opened
up my own English training company. I’ve been working hard ever since. My life seems to be one series of
entrepreneurial adventures – often involving taking
MASSIVE ACTION. And you know what? It’s sooo
much better!
Now I am not suggesting that you forget all about retirement and work hard just for the sake of working until
you drop dead. I think it’s all about making a contribution. If you don’t feel like you are contributing to the
betterment of others and enjoying what you do, then
you won’t be truly happy. Sure, drinking beer, watching
tv and playing golf is fun… for awhile. Then it isn’t.
So when you are planning for that day when you are
finally able to ‘retire’, take a look at what you can do to
stay productive and useful. You don’t have to do it full
time, but it should be a significant part of your life plan.
