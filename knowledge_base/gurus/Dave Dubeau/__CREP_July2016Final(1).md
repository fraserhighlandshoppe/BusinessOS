Anticipating Your Property
Management Needs
As the Real Estate investing expert your money partners will be looking to you for leadership, and managing the day-to-day operations of your properties. Sometimes you will need to hire a property management company and answers one of the biggest
question Real Estate investor have.
This month’s article comes from a guest contributor,
Brent Roberts who started investing at the tender age
of 18. Brent owns a portfolio of properties and has
been one of Vancouver’s top producing realtors since
1980.
As you invest in real estate, one of the questions you’ll
need to answer for yourself is, “Should I hire a property manager or should I do it myself?”
To help you answer that question, you need to first
accurately predict what kind of ongoing work will be
needed on your property. You can budget a predictable rate of 8% to 12% of gross rents for a property
management fee that will cover their basic services,
but what happens if something in the unit breaks and it
needs to be replaced? Accurately predicting what
could require repair or replacement will help you ensure that those situations can be addressed when they
arise, regardless of whether you are managing the
property yourself or outsourcing to a property manager.
Here are six areas to consider when anticipating your
property management needs:
1. House Age
All houses will require some regular up-keep as well as
regular upgrades. It should come as no surprise to you
that the age of the house will be a factor in the amount
of work you’ll need to maintain it. All equipment and
construction has a finite lifespan so investing in an older home may mean committing to replacing things that
have reached the end of their useful life sooner than
expected. However, that doesn’t mean that newer
houses are maintenance free. Newer houses may also
generate a higher than expected level of maintenance
as the house becomes lived in (i.e. windows stick,
doors don’t close properly, untested equipment starts
malfunctioning).
2. Lot Condition
The condition of the lot will also impact the type of
maintenance calls you’ll get. For example, trees on the
property can look beautiful but their leaves may clog
up your gutters while their roots may enter the property’s sewer lines. Or a rotting fence may fall over and

require clean-up and replacement.
3. Water
Consider how the property
handles water – both rainfall and snowmelt. Review
the grade of the lot to ensure that snowmelt or rainfall
runs away from the house in order to avoid getting
the dreaded water-in-the-basement calls. You’ll want
to make sure that sump pumps and backflow valves
are installed, but that may mean regular checks to
the property to ensure that they are functioning.
4. Neighbourhood
The neighbourhood will also play a part in the type of
calls you may get from your tenants. Some neighbourhoods might not add to the overall number of
calls you get while other neighbourhoods may increase the calls you get because of graffiti or vandalism. Get to know the neighbourhood so you can predict the frequency of these kinds of calls.
5. Local climate
Every season brings its own challenges for real estate investors, so it’s important to consider them all
equally. Spring and summer may bring increased
rainfall but fall and winter may bring increased snowfall. The local amounts of precipitation, temperature
fluctuations, and wind will all influence the kind of
calls you get. If you invest in a cold area, for example, where the furnace runs more often, the likelihood
of furnace calls will increase. If you invest in an area
that gets lots of snow, you will need to consider
whose responsibility it is to shovel the snow along
with regularly checking that snow build-up on the roof
doesn’t become dangerous.

THE TRUE COST OF A 2.5% FEE

Let’s assume that
you have $100,000
to invest and you
decide that you’re
going to invest in a mutual fund with strong
historical returns and a good management
team. This particular fund has generated
gross returns averaging 8% per year
(before fees) for the past several years and
charges a fairly typical MER of 2.5%. An
MER is an annual fee also called “trailer
fees” and is how the financial advisor is
compensated over time.
Following the advice of your financial planner (or as I say “salesperson”), you “buy
and hold” for a 20 year time period. As it
turns out, your mutual fund returns 8%
gross per year, just as you had hoped.
FIRST, let's look at what would have happened if you had invested that $100,000
but didn’t have to pay any fees. Your
$100,000 would have turned into
$466,095.71 (your original $100,000 plus
the return of $366,095.71) after twenty
years.
SECOND, let's look at what happens when
we include that little 2.5% management fee
into our calculations. Your $100,000 turns
into $280,910.14 ($100,000 plus the reduced return of $180,910.14). Due to the
2.5% MER fee, the difference between the
two is almost $185,000.
You’ve lost about 50% of your potential returns simply because of a 2.5% fee.
There are two places that your returns are
diluted substantially. FIRST, the 2.5% fee
actually ends up costing you 25.2% of your
returns because every year, the 2.5% fee
is charged against your entire investment
account, even though you earn 8%.

Page 4

The reality is that you’re not incurring a 2.5%
fee each year, you’re actually paying out
2.5% of the 8% total returns that you made
that year. This means the MER is actually
costing you more than 31% of your total investment returns (2.5 / 8 x 100 = 31.25%).
SECOND, every time the MER is removed
from your account, you’ve lost the potential
earnings you could have made from compounding had that money remained invested.
The 2.5% that you paid out in fees last year is
not in your account to work for you this year,
and over time this creates a significant loss of
25.4% because of those expenses being deducted and reducing the benefit of compound
returns for you.
This example assumes a consistent 8% gross
return by the mutual fund over a 20 year time
period. The results would be much worse if
the fund earned a lower growth rate, or negative by losing money.

Do I Sell Or Do I Refinance?
When it comes time to raise capital or divest yourself of
some properties, the quick and easy solution is to simply sell the property. The challenge is that there are
many different issues to take into consideration when
selling, with, the main one being taxation.
Now if you need to divest yourself of your property for
health reasons or you simply want to get out of the
business of being a landlord then the obvious move is
to sell and pay the consequences. The consequences I
am referring to is the “tax man”. How much tax you will
need to pay will depend upon many things such as the
capital growth of the property, recapture of depreciation, your personal/corporation tax rate, amongst other
things.
If your plan is to grow your portfolio then selling a small
property to buy a bigger property may not be the best
option. When you sell an investment property in Canada you must pay a capital gains tax plus you must repay Revenue Canada all of the tax savings you took
advantage of through depreciation (assuming you depreciated the property). I am sure you may have heard
someone say “They can’t afford to sell”. What they
mean by this is that they would have to pay the government so much in taxes that there would be no profit left
over so they simply keep the property. In the US market Capital Gains is handled differently because when
you sell a property you can avoid paying taxes on the
Capital Gain as long as you reinvest in another property of equal or greater value within six months. Otherwise you will need to pay taxes on the Capital Gains,
just as we do in Canada.
These types of issues are what you need to be aware
of when you are creating your investment plan. Personally we do not depreciate any property that we know we
will be selling within the next 5-10 years. This doesn’t
mean you can’t or shouldn’t depreciate your property it
simply means you need to be aware of the tax implications when you do sell it.
Let’s get back to growing your portfolio and the need to
access the equity in the property. Instead of selling the
property what if you were to refinance the property or
place a second mortgage on the property? These are
both very good strategies to access equity that has
grown in the property without triggering any taxes.
There are no taxes involved when you borrow money
simply because you only pay tax on earned income
and “borrowed” money is not earned income, it is borrowed money.
Option #1 would be to refinance the property using
the current lender or a new lender. The first thing you
should realize is that a mortgage on a property larger
than 4 units will be considered a commercial mortgage
and the rules for commercial mortgages are very differ-

ent from those for a residential mortgage. With a residential mortgage you can often
pay out the mortgage early if
you wish to and pay a modest
penalty for doing so. This is
not an option with commercial mortgages. If you
want to pay out the mortgage early then the only way
to do so is to pay the lender the entire balance of
interest owing (ouch!!). This means that the only time
you can really consider refinancing the first mortgage
is when the mortgage comes up for renewal which
may or may not be at the right time for you. Another
challenge of refinancing with the lender of the first
mortgage is that they may require you to requalify
even though you went through the qualifying process
the first time. From the lenders perspective things
may have changed financially with the property and
you so they want to make sure everything is still
good. The bigger issue about requalifying is the potential of rule changes which may make it more difficult for you to qualify or for the property to qualify. A
good example of this would be the requirement of an
environmental study to determine if there are any
potential environmental concerns. Perhaps they didn’t require one when you bought the property but
now they do. There is the additional cost to have the
study completed and then there is the risk of discovering something which could cost you thousands of
dollars to remediate and it may cause the lender to
say no to lending!.
Option #2 is actually my preference these days
and this is to leave the existing first mortgage alone
and place secondary financing on the property. Depending upon the source you may still only be able to
finance up to 80% of the property value; however,
there are sources out there that allow up to 90% of
the property value. One of the first push backs I hear
from Borrowers with respect to secondary financing
is the high cost.

Ride the Wave of Success!
No doubt there have been times in
your life when you felt like you were
‘in the flow’. When gamblers experience a winning streak they say they
are ‘on a roll’. Athletes and performers
call it ‘being in the zone’. As a coach I
call it ‘riding a wave of success’. These are all the same
– times when you feel momentum as if you are being
carried along by a favorable current.
You’ve no doubt experienced the opposite, too. A string
of misfortune when everything seems to be falling apart
and it feels like you are fighting your way upstream.
You may consider these situations involve good or bad
luck but you have more control over them than you
might think. If you pay attention there are actions you
can take to create, increase and sustain a wave of success or diminish and end a losing streak.
Let’s start with the positive. How do you initiate, expand
or extend a run of good fortune?
You start by paying attention. Recall a time from the past
when you were on a roll and reconnect with how it felt in
your body. Most likely the wave gave you a sense of
momentum, optimism and invincibility. You can use that
feeling to boost an existing wave of success or even create a new one.
When you get good news or notice something favorable,
interpret it as a sign that things are going your
way. Welcome it into your life and feel the wave building. Celebrate this new wave of success and embrace
the thrill of you increasing momentum. We are all surrounded with blessings and positive occurrences all the
time but our negative attitudes and expectations can
block us from seeing them. To change the tide, start noticing the positive occurrences that are happening for
you.
Now expand the scope of your attention. Any time you
notice or hear of something positive happening in the
world, embrace it and feel your momentum increase.
When you are riding a wave of success avoid negative

thoughts and feelings. Dwelling on negativity will
quickly snuff out your wave. By all means be aware if
what is going on around you but don’t allow the media,
gossip or your own pessimistic attitude to pull you
down.
If you are experiencing a losing streak, the key to
eliminating it is to take what is happening in stride
without blaming others or yourself. Instead of catastrophizing, keep things in perspective. The fastest way to
turn things around is to focus on what you have and
what is working.
Another factor that makes a genuine difference in both
positive and negative circumstances is gratitude. If
you identify and celebrate things you are grateful for
when you are riding a wave of success, the wave is
likely to expand and last longer. If things are going
badly for you, the fastest way to turn things around is
to be grateful for what you already have.
I’m not suggesting that you be a Pollyanna and block
anything that is negative. What I am pointing out is
that the momentum you have in your life, whether it is
positive or negative, is largely created by your thinking
– and you can control what you think about. When
Harv Eker and I were business partners he used to
say, “Where attention goes, energy flows and results
show.” By all means acknowledge the negative but
don’t wallow in it. When you consciously focus on positive thoughts it really does make a difference.
This may all sound weird and magical to you but I am
sharing this based on real world experience. The reason it works is because in life we get what we focus
on and expect. It’s not that bad things can’t happen
when you are feeling good; obviously they can. But
when times are challenging, focusing on the positive
elements turns things around faster. And when things
are going your way, gratitude and celebration will increase your wave of success.

Private Capital Markets
The largest ever inter-generational transfer of wealth
will occur over the next decade as Canadian Baby
Boomers are poised to inherit billions. CIBC Inheritance Survey estimates that about 2.5 million Canadians over the age of 75 have a combined net worth
of over $900 Billion. An even bigger amount of $30
Trillion is the estimated wealth transfer that will occur
in the United States over the next few decades, as
conducted by consulting firm Accenture. The main
source of this wealth is in real estate.
I firmly believe that everyone should own real estate
instead of mutual funds or equities. But I do understand that self-managing a real estate portfolio is not
for everyone. This is where the experts in exempt
market can be a lucrative alternative.
Unlike publicly traded shares on the stock exchange,
private markets are where non-public companies
raise capital from investors under a prospectus exemption. Under Canadian Securities law, a company
can raise capital through a prospectus offering in the
public market or privately under a prospectus exemption.
A prospectus exemption means that the company is
not required to satisfy the prospectus requirements
and can rely on alternative means of raising capital.
This private market is called the exempt market.
More capital was raised in Canada in the private markets than in the public markets. In 2014, over 90% of
capital raised in Canada was in the private markets
under the Accredited Investor Prospectus Exemption.
Accredited investors are typically wealthy Canadians
who comprise about 4% of the population, and they
must satisfy one of three financial tests to be eligible
to invest:
1: financial asset test
an individual who, either alone or with a spouse, beneficially owns, directly or indirectly, financial assets
having an aggregate realizable value that before taxes, but net of any related liabilities, exceeds
$1,000,000; (this does not include primary residence), or,
2: income test
an individual whose net income before taxes exceeded $200,000 in each of the two most recent years or
whose net income before taxes combined with that of
a spouse exceeded $300,000 in each of the two most
recent calendar years and who, in either case, reasonably expects to exceed that net income level in
the current year, or,
3: net asset test

an individual who, either
alone or with a spouse,
has net assets of at least
$5,000,000; (this includes primary residence)
Accredited Investors invest in the private market because higher risk equates to higher reward. These types
of investments are also illiquid because they are not
traded on the public stock exchange where stocks can
be freely traded, bought and sold on a daily basis.
For example, consider you are an accredited investor
and have $100,000 available to invest. You could
choose to invest in a Guaranteed Investment Certificate
(GIC) or a Term Deposit earning interest ranging from
0.6% to 2.0% interest (locked or unlocked term). The
interest rates are low because the risk is low. The alternative private market investment offers a 100% return
on equity over a three-year period that equates to an
annualized return of 33% per year. Non-accredited investors which comprise the majority of Canadians, are
not eligible to invest in the higher risk investments, unless you are a close family member, close friend or
close business associate of the person selling the investment.
In 1933, the Securities and Exchange Commission
(SEC) was created by the government to regulate private investments because there were so many investors
being taken advantage of. One of the SEC’s first laws
was to prevent non-accredited investors from investing
in private deals in order to protect the unsophisticated
investor. But in doing so, the majority of investors were
now limited to safe, low yield investments.
As you can see from this, the government is in part the
reason for why the rich get richer. So why doesn’t an
exempt market issuer take money from a non-accredited
investor anyway?

Buyer Beware! – New Home
Construction Deal Woes
I would like to share a story of a
recent investment experience that
didn’t go so well. The lessons learned could be very
valuable to anyone facing a similar situation in the future. Back in January of this year one of my clients at
the time met with a local home Builder. It was negotiated that Mr. Client would purchase one of the last units
on the block. It appeared to be a win win where the
property made sense from a monthly cash flow basis.
It also helped clear out the last of the builder’s inventory. A contract was delivered to my office in order to acquire the financing. New home builds take up to 10
months to finish building, so the financing is a bit of a
challenge. The reason is that holding today’s very low
rates and approval for that long time. Unless the financing is specifically arranged with the builder and the
lender, most will only hold the financing approvals for 3
to 4 months. (There are a few banks that approve
mortgages for longer durations, usually at higher rates).
In this case, Mr and Mrs Client lost one of their two incomes due to economic cut backs. They were nervous
about the lower income and they approached me and
asked “Are we able to just walk from this deal?”
I gave them my opinion and let them know they should
consult a good real estate lawyer to see if their agreement allowed for the contract to be broken due to income loss. At this point there were thousands of dollars
on deposit with the builder. Legally the purchase contract had no provisions for this type of issue. The real
estate lawyer told Mr. and Mrs. Client that not only
would they lose all of their deposit money, they could
also be sued for damages! This could happen if they
backed out and the builder had to sell it on the open
market for less than their agreed upon price.
In the end once they discovered that not going through
with the purchase would cause them more of a financial loss than to actually buying the home, they decided
to move ahead.
By that time there were only a few weeks before the
whole mortgage was to be funded. In an effort to assist
the reactivated deal our office pulled out some favors
and got the deal reapproved at a very low competitive
rate.
To complicate things even more, Mr. and Mrs. Client
had several years of back taxes that were owed to the
CRA that they failed to disclose. The lender insisted
that this be paid before they funded their mortgage
money.

Page 8

A second issue was due to the longer closing timeframe for a new home. They had two appraisals
done, and both showed the current market value to
be ten thousand dollars less than the original agreed
purchase price!
The bank dropped their eighty percent financing to
the lower appraised value and not the original price.
This forced Mr. and Mrs. Client to put in the extra
money themselves. Even more cash OUT at this
point.
What are the lessons here?
-Always buy good cash flowing properties that fit
your investment strategy (in good times or bad
times). This is a must.
-Know the risks that come with purchasing from a
new home builder.
- Do your best to lock in the interest rate and the
terms of your original mortgage approval.
- Remember that all mortgage approvals state that
there can be NO material change in your financial
picture. Know that most banks will recheck incomes, any new debts and house values immediately before closing.
- Be sure to consult with your broker about any potential snag in income or other issues right away to
review what your options may or may not be.
-If you are self-employed know that owing any
amount of back taxes to the CRA is a show stopper
with the tier 1 banks. There are lenders who will still

Five Questions to Ask Yourself
Before Taking the Plunge
If you are considering buying an investment property,
there are several things you should take into consideration such as cost, location and so much more.
Making the choice to buy an investment property is a
huge decision, and before you make the jump make
sure you’ve done the right research to support your
decision.
Here are my top five questions that any potential real
estate investor should be asking themselves before
they sign on the dotted line.
1. What’s the rental potential? Just because your realtor says a property will earn
$400 a week in rent, doesn’t mean it will. Do
your research and look at what rentals are
going for in your location to get a realistic
idea of rents on properties similar to the one
you want to purchase. Another good idea is
to ask an independent company to do a rental appraisal. Properties are often under or
overpriced and a rental appraisal will give
you a more accurate picture.
2.

What’s the current market price?
Many buyers sometimes get carried away
with the idea that they have to pay below
market price in order for it to be considered a
good investment. However, that is not necessarily the case. Sometimes buying the “right”
property means paying the current market
value or sometimes more to make it a first
class, long term investment.

Amend Your Mortgage: All mortgages are amendable; some because you and the lender might negotiate

monthly payments. As part of any reorganization, you could ask or some lenders may offer to add your missed
payments to the principal balance of your mortgage. That would spread out those missed payments over the balance of your mortgage.

some form of relief by way of forgiven missed payments or interest-only payments. Check your paperwork and if

Lessons From A Barber Shop.

I recently visited my friend Michael McLean for
several days at his lakeside cottage near Perth,
Ontario. He showed me his newest business venture, an old-fashioned barber shop called “Father
and Sons”. Michael has absolutely zero previous
experience in this industry, and became enthralled
by the idea after visiting a very cool and successful
barber when he was on a business trip to the USA
a couple of years ago.
His new shop has only been open for a year, and it
is already a very well known (and successful) feature of Perth.
He’s accomplished this by applying very smart
marketing and showmanship to this new business.
I had a haircut and a hot-towel shave at the shop,
and while I enjoyed the experience very much, I’m
always more interested in the behind-the-scenes
things that make a successful business tick.
Here are some of the ideas I took from this business.
Improve, but don’t re-invent the wheel. Barber
shops have been around for thousands of
years. Michael found one he liked in the States,

took lots of notes, and researched other supersuccessful barber shops before he got serious about
opening his. He took everything he liked from all the
different shops and applied it to his own.
Hire and Train the BEST Staff you can. Michael
hired away a very experienced barber from a competitor in town. In addition to an attractive salary, he
gave the lady a small ownership stake in the company (as well as future franchising of it). He did the
same thing with a general manager – giving the GM
from his insurance business an ownership interest in
the new business in exchange for her managing the
day-to-day operations (on her own time, outside of
the insurance company hours). Michael also hired a
top-notch barber-trainer to show his staff how to best
cut hair and do shaves.
Keep it simple and focused. This is not a hair salon. It is a barber shop that caters exclusively to
men and boys who want hair cuts. There’s no colouring, perms, etc. His marketing is targeted to men
(and wives and mothers) who are looking for good,
simple, fast hair cuts.
