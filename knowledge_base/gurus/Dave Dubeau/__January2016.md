BALANCING ACT with Randall Orser

4 Old Lessons New Entrepreneurs Can Use
Being an entrepreneur is difficult and often thankless. Fortunately, you're not the first
one to go down this road. You've joined a long history of people who had an idea,
believed that people would pay for it, and founded a start-up based on that belief.
One of the best things you can do is to learn from those who came before you. That way you can avoid
their mistakes and give yourself a leg-up in terms of success.
1. Trust Your Instincts
Your instincts usually will lead you in the right direction. There's an old test-taking strategy that involves
going with your first choice. It prevents choice paralysis and spurs you into action, which is important.
The fact is you can only spend so much time thinking and doing research. Go with your gut. Naturally,
you should only trust your instincts when you're not sure which option to take, but have enough
knowledge to figure out which options are better than the rest.
This can also be applied to people. If you don't think they're being completely honest, or you feel a
supplier can give you more, keep pushing. Just make sure to change your tune if they insist that they can't
do more.
2. Loyalty Counts for a Lot
Money is not the only thing that motivates people, despite what some TV shows may want you to believe.
Loyalty exists and that loyalty can enable your business relationships to survive rocky moments.
Employees, suppliers, and business partners alike are fundamentally looking out for their best interests;
with loyalty, they will view their interests as tied to yours, even when things look bad.
Loyalty is a two-way street. If you exhibit yourself as someone willing to throw people under the bus
when it suits you, you'll never inspire loyalty in others. Keep promises and back people up as often as
possible. This also goes for how you treat your customers. If you promise them something, such as
frequent product updates and innovations, deliver.
3. Things are Better Than They Seem
As an entrepreneur, you're going to face a lot of pressure, internal and external. Your partners and
investors want you to meet certain metrics and produce the right results. From within, you face the fear of
failing and losing everything you've worked for, which can lead to a number of sleepless nights. You
might even find your palms sweaty at the thought of a
struggle -- if it's such a good idea, shouldn't things be
going well, or did you make a mistake somewhere?

More often than not, it only feels like things are going poorly. The fact is some part of your venture will
be a struggle, and it won't always be the same thing. Something will always be wrong, but that doesn't
mean the start-up is failing. It's just part of how things go as an entrepreneur.
4. Always be Respectful
You're going to meet a lot of people as an entrepreneur and it's going to get tiring. Even the most avid
social butterfly will want a little alone time at some point, but alone time is not always up to them.
Sometimes you have to meet with someone, even though all you want to do is sit at home with a mug of
cocoa. Despite this, you must remain respectful and responsive to everyone you meet, even solicitors.
There are benefits to being polite and responsive, but that's not the primary reason for you to do it. It's
easy to start seeing people in terms of positives and negatives and how they can benefit your company. If
you forget that you're dealing with people, you will soon lose touch, not just with potential partners, but
with your customers. When that happens, you'll have trouble deciding how to develop your product.
These lessons are timeless and useful to any entrepreneur, no matter when you start your journey. Loyalty
will always matter, regardless of what industry you're in. Treating people well will never go out of style
and will remind you of whom you're ultimately serving. Remembering that things are better than they
seem will let you keep your chin up and keep on trucking, even when things look bleak. These are far
from the only things you can learn from your predecessors, but they're enough to get you started.
